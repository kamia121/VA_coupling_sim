Concepts

The ventricle and its arterial load can both be expressed as elastances in mmHg/mL. Together with the end-diastolic volume, their ratio determines stroke volume and ejection fraction, and it governs how efficiently the heart uses energy.

The pressure–volume loop

The loop runs counter-clockwise. The ventricle fills along the bottom, contracts at constant volume up the right side, ejects across the top, and relaxes at constant volume down the left side. The width of the loop is the stroke volume, and its area is the stroke work.

Ees and the contractility of the ventricle

During systole the ventricle behaves like a spring that stiffens as it contracts. At any instant, the pressure it generates equals its stiffness at that moment multiplied by the volume it holds above an unstressed volume, V₀, so that P(t) = E(t) × (V − V₀). The stiffness E(t) is called elastance and is expressed in mmHg/mL. It rises through systole and reaches its maximum at end-systole. That maximum is Ees, the end-systolic elastance .

Ees is the pressure the ventricle can hold at end-systole for each milliliter it still contains above V₀. A ventricle with a high Ees ejects down to a small end-systolic volume even against a high pressure, whereas a ventricle with a low Ees stops ejecting while it is still large. On the pressure–volume loop, end-systole is the upper left corner, where the aortic valve closes in the LV and the pulmonic valve closes in the RV. When preload or afterload changes over a few beats, this corner moves, but the corners of all the loops stay on one straight line. That line is the end-systolic pressure–volume relation (ESPVR). Its slope is Ees, and it crosses the volume axis at V₀. The RV behaves the same way .

Because the ESPVR barely moves when the load changes, Ees measures contractility in a way that EF cannot. EF falls when afterload rises even if the muscle itself is unchanged. Ees rises with inotropes and sympathetic drive and falls with ischemia, cardiomyopathy, or septic myocardial depression . Concentric hypertrophy also raises Ees, because a thick, stiff ventricle has a steep ESPVR . A high Ees is therefore not always a sign of a healthy heart. A dilated, failing ventricle often has a flatter ESPVR that is also shifted to the right, with a larger V₀. Filling follows a separate, curved relation, the end-diastolic pressure–volume relation (EDPVR). The EDPVR describes diastolic stiffness and is independent of Ees.

Ea and the load from the arteries

Ea, the effective arterial elastance, describes the load against which the ventricle ejects, in the same units as Ees. Ea is the rise in end-systolic pressure for each milliliter the ventricle ejects, or Ea = Pes / SV . Ea is not the stiffness of any single vessel. It combines the resistance, the compliance, and the wave reflections of the whole arterial tree into one number. In patients, Ea measured from pressure–volume loops agreed with Ea calculated from a three-element Windkessel model of the arterial tree .

On the pressure–volume loop, Ea is drawn as a line that starts at end-diastolic volume on the volume axis, where pressure is zero, and rises to the end-systolic corner. The slope of that line is −Ea, and a steeper line means a heavier load. The largest single influence on Ea is resistance divided by the duration of the beat, so that Ea ≈ SVR / T. Ea tracked SVR/T closely (r 0.97–0.99), and pulsatile load mattered less . Heart rate matters because at a fast rate the arteries have less time to empty between beats, and each milliliter ejected leaves the pressure higher. Ea therefore rises with vasoconstriction, hypertension, and tachycardia, and it falls with vasodilation and vasoplegia. At the bedside, Ea can be estimated as 0.9 × systolic pressure / stroke volume .

The ratio and where the two lines meet

At the end of systole, the ventricle and its load must agree. The end-systolic point has to lie on the ESPVR, which gives the pressure the ventricle can hold at each volume, and it also has to lie on the Ea line, which gives the pressure the arteries generate for each stroke volume. The only point that satisfies both is where the two lines cross. That crossing sets the end-systolic volume and pressure, and with them the stroke volume. Ees and Ea together determine how far the ventricle empties.

SV = (EDV − V₀) × Ees / (Ees + Ea)  ⇒  EF ≈ 1 / (1 + Ea/Ees)

The formula can be read as a division of volume. EDV − V₀ is the largest volume the ventricle could eject. The ventricle ejects the fraction Ees/(Ees + Ea) of that volume and keeps the rest. When Ees equals Ea, the ventricle ejects half of that volume; when Ees is twice Ea, it ejects two thirds; and when Ea is twice Ees, it ejects only one third. EF follows almost directly from this fraction, which is why EF behaves more like an index of coupling than a measure of contractility. At the normal Ea/Ees of about 0.6, EF is about 60%.

The same fraction explains why a weak ventricle is so sensitive to afterload. A 50% rise in Ea reduces the ejected fraction by about 16% when Ees is normal at 2.3 mmHg/mL, but by about 24% when Ees is 0.8 mmHg/mL, as in a failing heart. For the same reason, afterload reduction helps a weak ventricle more than a normal one. In the simulator, the formula predicts the model's stroke volume within about 1 mL.

Derivation and its assumptions

End-systole lies on the ESPVR, so Pes = Ees × (ESV − V₀). By the definition of Ea, the same point also satisfies Pes = Ea × SV = Ea × (EDV − ESV). Setting the two expressions equal gives Ees × (ESV − V₀) = Ea × (EDV − ESV). Solving for ESV gives ESV = (Ea × EDV + Ees × V₀) / (Ees + Ea). Stroke volume is then SV = EDV − ESV = Ees × (EDV − V₀) / (Ees + Ea), and end-systolic pressure is Pes = Ea × SV = [Ees × Ea / (Ees + Ea)] × (EDV − V₀).

Dividing SV by EDV gives EF = [Ees / (Ees + Ea)] × (1 − V₀/EDV). When V₀ is small relative to EDV, EF ≈ 1/(1 + Ea/Ees). The same algebra also gives SV/(ESV − V₀) = Ees/Ea, which is the basis for using SV/ESV as a volume estimate of Ees/Ea when V₀ is close to zero.

Ea is approximately SVR/T for the following reason. Over one beat, mean flow is SV/T. If the arterial load were purely resistive, mean pressure would be SVR × SV/T. Taking end-systolic pressure as approximately equal to mean pressure gives Pes/SV ≈ SVR/T. Compliance and wave reflection add to this value, but only modestly .

The derivation assumes that the ESPVR is linear over the working range, that Ea is constant from beat to beat, and that EDV is fixed. In the body, EDV depends on venous return and on the other ventricle, so a change in Ea also shifts EDV. The simulator includes this effect, and the formula does not.

Energy use has its own optimum. Stroke work is greatest when Ea equals Ees, and efficiency is greatest at a lower Ea . In isolated canine hearts, stroke work peaked at an Ea/Ees of 0.80 and efficiency at 0.70, and both remained at 90% or more of their optimum between 0.3 and 1.3 . Normal human hearts operate at an Ees/Ea of 1.62, which corresponds to an Ea/Ees of about 0.62 , near the efficient end of that range.

A normal ratio can hide disease

The ratio reduces two numbers to one. When Ees and Ea rise or fall together, the ratio stays in the normal range even though pressures, volumes, or flow are abnormal. In patients with HFpEF, Ees averaged 4.7 and Ea 2.6 mmHg/mL, for a ratio of about 0.55 . The simulator shows the normal range beside the ratio, but the ratio should always be read together with the absolute values of Ees and Ea, the filling pressure, and the cardiac output.

Stroke work and PVA

The pressure–volume area (PVA) is the sum of the stroke work and the potential energy, which is the area bounded by the ESPVR, the EDPVR, and the relaxation limb of the loop. In canine hearts, PVA was linearly related to myocardial oxygen consumption per beat . The ratio SW/PVA is the mechanical efficiency. The simulator approximates the potential energy as ½ × Pes × (ESV − V₀).

The right ventricle

The RV has a thin wall and ejects into a low-resistance, high-compliance circulation. Its loop is trapezoidal or triangular, and ejection continues while pressure is already falling. Pulmonary resistance and compliance are inversely related, and their product, the RC time, is similar across patients and stays constant with treatment . In CTEPH it measured 0.49 ± 0.2 s . As a consequence, a small rise in PVR from normal brings a large fall in compliance.

RV coupling is usually reported as Ees/Ea, with a normal value of 1.5 to 2 . In chronic pressure overload, the RV first adapts homeometrically, raising Ees through hypertrophy while its volumes are preserved, and later heterometrically, by dilating . RV dilation and failure were associated with an Ees/Ea below 0.805 . On MRI, Emax/Ea was 1.1 ± 0.3 in pulmonary hypertension and 1.9 ± 0.4 in controls . Because the ventricles work in series, a low RV output lowers LV preload. In patients, septal shift and pericardial constraint add to this effect; the model does not include them .

Model

P(V,t) = e(t)·Ees·(V − V₀) + [1 − e(t)]·A·(eβ(V − V₀) − 1)

The model has eight compartments, which are the two ventricles, the two atria, the systemic and pulmonary arteries (each a three-element Windkessel), and the passive systemic and pulmonary veins. The ventricular activation function e(t) is a normalized double-Hill function . Each atrium is also a time-varying elastance. Its contraction begins at the P wave, 160 ms before ventricular activation, and lasts 140 ms, which produces the a wave and the late-diastolic filling of the ventricle, the atrial kick. The valves are ideal. The equations are solved by a fourth-order Runge–Kutta method with 0.5 ms steps until the beats reach a steady state. Ea is measured from the beat, using Pes at peak elastance, rather than set as an input. Model Pes is about 0.97 × SBP, so model Ea runs about 8% above the bedside estimate of 0.9 × SBP/SV.

Calibration and limits

The normal model has an EF of 58%, a blood pressure of 119/73 mmHg, a cardiac output of 5.7 L/min, a LAP of 7 mmHg, and an LV Ea/Ees of 0.62 . Its mPAP is 14 mmHg, compared with 14.0 ± 3.3 mmHg in healthy subjects , its PVR is 1.2 WU, and its RV Ees/Ea is 2.0. Atrial contraction supplies 17% of the stroke volume, and removing it, as in atrial fibrillation, lowers the stroke volume by 9% in the normal heart and by 14% in the HFpEF scenario.

The model does not include ventricular interdependence, the pericardium, valve disease, the c wave, the effect of the descending base on atrial pressure, the baroreflex, the force–frequency relation, coronary flow, or the time constant of relaxation. Without reflexes, pressures in shock and in acute PE run lower than in patients. An R implementation of the same equations is in the repository.
