Concepts

The ventricle and its arterial load can both be expressed as elastances in mmHg/mL. Together with end-diastolic volume, the ratio of these two elastances determines stroke volume and ejection fraction and sets the energetic efficiency of ejection.

The pressure–volume loop

The loop runs counterclockwise. The ventricle fills along the diastolic limb at the bottom, contracts isovolumically up the right side, ejects across the top, and relaxes isovolumically down the left side. The width of the loop is the stroke volume, and its enclosed area is the external stroke work.

End-systolic elastance (Ees)

During systole the ventricle behaves as a spring that stiffens as it contracts. At any instant, the pressure it generates equals its instantaneous stiffness multiplied by the volume it holds above an unstressed volume, V₀, such that P(t) = E(t) × (V − V₀). This time-varying stiffness, or elastance, is expressed in mmHg/mL; it rises through systole and reaches its maximum at end-systole, where it is termed the end-systolic elastance, Ees .

Ees is the pressure the ventricle sustains at end-systole for each milliliter it contains above V₀. A ventricle with high Ees empties to a small end-systolic volume even against high afterload, whereas a ventricle with low Ees terminates ejection early at a much larger volume. On the pressure–volume loop, end-systole is the upper left corner, where the aortic valve closes in the LV and the pulmonic valve closes in the RV. When preload or afterload is altered over a few beats this corner moves, yet the end-systolic corners of successive loops remain on a single straight line, the end-systolic pressure–volume relation (ESPVR), whose slope is Ees and whose volume intercept is V₀. The RV obeys the same relation .

Because the ESPVR is relatively insensitive to acute loading conditions, Ees reflects the inotropic and geometric properties of the ventricle more reliably than ejection fraction does. EF declines with increasing afterload even when myocardial function is preserved. Ees, in contrast, increases with inotropic stimulation or sympathetic tone and decreases with ischemia, chronic cardiomyopathy, or septic myocardial depression . A steep ESPVR can also arise from concentric hypertrophy , so a high Ees does not necessarily indicate a healthy heart. At the other extreme, ventricular dilation yields a flattened, rightward-shifted relation with a larger volume intercept. Passive filling follows a separate curvilinear boundary, the end-diastolic pressure–volume relation (EDPVR), which describes diastolic stiffness independently of Ees.

Effective arterial elastance (Ea)

Effective arterial elastance, Ea, expresses the load against which the ventricle ejects in the same units as Ees. It is defined as the end-systolic pressure generated per milliliter ejected, Ea = Pes / SV . Ea is not the stiffness of any single vessel; it was proposed as a lumped index of the resistance, compliance, and wave reflections of the entire arterial tree. In patients, Ea measured from pressure–volume loops agreed with Ea calculated from a three-element Windkessel model of the arterial tree .

On the pressure–volume loop, Ea is represented by a line running from the end-diastolic volume on the volume axis, where pressure is zero, up to the end-systolic point. Because this line rises from right to left, its slope is −Ea, and a steeper line denotes a heavier afterload. The dominant determinant of Ea is systemic vascular resistance normalized to the duration of the cardiac cycle, Ea ≈ SVR / T. In more than 2,500 adults, SVR and heart rate explained 96% to 98% of the variability in Ea, Ea correlated with SVR/T at r = 0.97 to 0.99, and the pulsatile components of load explained less than 1% . Heart rate matters because a shorter cycle truncates diastolic runoff and leaves a higher residual arterial pressure for each subsequent stroke volume. Consequently, Ea rises with vasoconstriction, hypertension, and tachycardia and falls with vasodilation and vasoplegia. At the bedside, Ea can be approximated as 0.9 × systolic blood pressure / stroke volume .

The Ea/Ees ratio

At the end of systole, the ventricle and the arterial system reach mechanical equilibrium. The end-systolic point must lie simultaneously on the ESPVR, which gives the pressure the ventricle can sustain at each volume, and on the Ea line, which gives the arterial pressure generated by ejecting each stroke volume. The only operating point that satisfies both constraints is the intersection of the two lines. This crossing sets the end-systolic volume and pressure and thereby fixes the stroke volume of the beat.

SV = (EDV − V₀) × Ees / (Ees + Ea)  ⇒  EF ≈ 1 / (1 + Ea/Ees)

The formula partitions the available volume. The term EDV − V₀ is the theoretical maximum volume the ventricle could eject against zero afterload, and the fraction Ees / (Ees + Ea) determines how much of that volume is discharged and how much is retained. When Ees equals Ea, the ventricle ejects half of this volume; when Ees is twice Ea, it ejects two thirds; and when Ea is twice Ees, ejection falls to one third. Because EF tracks this fraction so closely, it behaves primarily as an index of ventricular–arterial coupling rather than as a measure of intrinsic contractility. At a typical resting Ea/Ees of 0.6 to 0.7, EF is near 60%.

The failing ventricle is disproportionately sensitive to afterload for the same reason. With a normal Ees of 2.3 mmHg/mL, a 50% increase in Ea reduces the ejected fraction by about 16%; with an Ees of 0.8 mmHg/mL, as in a failing ventricle, the same increase reduces it by about 24%. By the same mechanism, afterload reduction yields a larger gain in stroke volume in a failing ventricle than in a normal one. In the simulator, the arterial vasodilator (SVR × 0.7) raises stroke volume by about 19% in the HFrEF scenario and by about 8% in the normal heart, and the formula itself predicts the simulated stroke volume to within about 1 mL.

Derivation and its assumptions

End-systole lies on the ESPVR, so Pes = Ees × (ESV − V₀). By the definition of Ea, the same point also satisfies Pes = Ea × SV = Ea × (EDV − ESV). Equating the two expressions gives Ees × (ESV − V₀) = Ea × (EDV − ESV), and solving for ESV gives ESV = (Ea × EDV + Ees × V₀) / (Ees + Ea). Stroke volume is then SV = EDV − ESV = Ees × (EDV − V₀) / (Ees + Ea), and end-systolic pressure is Pes = Ea × SV = [Ees × Ea / (Ees + Ea)] × (EDV − V₀).

Dividing SV by EDV gives EF = [Ees / (Ees + Ea)] × (1 − V₀/EDV). When V₀ is small relative to EDV, EF ≈ 1/(1 + Ea/Ees). The same algebra also gives SV/(ESV − V₀) = Ees/Ea, which is the basis for using SV/ESV as a volume estimate of Ees/Ea when V₀ is close to zero.

Over one beat, mean flow is SV/T. If the arterial load were purely resistive, mean pressure would be SVR × SV/T, and taking end-systolic pressure as approximately equal to mean pressure gives Pes/SV ≈ SVR/T. Compliance and wave reflection add only modestly to this value .

The derivation assumes that the ESPVR is linear over the working range, that Ea is constant from beat to beat, and that EDV is fixed. In the intact circulation, EDV depends on venous return and on the output of the other ventricle, so a change in Ea also shifts EDV; the simulator includes this interaction, whereas the formula does not.

Stroke work is maximal when Ea equals Ees, whereas mechanical efficiency is maximal at a lower relative afterload . In ten isolated, blood-perfused canine hearts, stroke work peaked at an Ea/Ees of 0.80 and efficiency at 0.70, and both remained at 90% or more of their maximal values over an Ea/Ees range of 0.3 to 1.3, which corresponds to ejection fractions of about 40% to 80% . At rest, the normal human left ventricle operates at an Ees/Ea of 1.62, or an Ea/Ees of about 0.62 . This operating point is consistent with the analytical prediction that ventricular and arterial properties are matched toward efficiency rather than toward maximal stroke work .

Disease with a normal ratio

When Ees and Ea increase or decrease in parallel, the ratio remains normal despite marked derangements in pressure, chamber volume, and forward flow. In patients with HFpEF, Ees and Ea averaged 4.7 and 2.6 mmHg/mL, a ratio of about 0.55 that coexisted with ventricular and arterial stiffening . The coupling ratio must therefore be interpreted alongside the absolute elastances, the filling pressures, and the cardiac output. In the simulator, the HFpEF scenario has an LV Ea/Ees of 0.50 with a LAP of 16 mmHg and a MAP of 139 mmHg; a 150-mL fluid bolus raises LAP by 3.2 mmHg, compared with 2.3 mmHg in the normal heart, and the arterial vasodilator lowers MAP by 30 mmHg, compared with 21 mmHg.

Stroke work and pressure–volume area

The pressure–volume area (PVA) is the sum of the external stroke work and the elastic potential energy stored at end-systole, which is the area bounded by the ESPVR, the EDPVR, and the isovolumic relaxation limb of the loop. In canine left ventricles contracting under a variety of loading conditions, PVA correlated linearly with myocardial oxygen consumption per beat (r = 0.89) . The quotient SW/PVA is the mechanical efficiency of the beat, the fraction of the total mechanical energy converted into external work. The simulator approximates the potential energy with the triangle ½ × Pes × (ESV − V₀), and the SW/PVA readout moves with every change of Ees, Ea, or volume.

The right ventricle

Unlike the thick-walled left ventricle, the normal RV is a thin, crescentic chamber that ejects into a low-resistance, high-compliance vascular bed. Because pulmonary impedance is low, its pressure–volume loop is trapezoidal or triangular, and ejection continues after intracavitary pressure has begun to decline. Pulmonary resistance and compliance are inversely and hyperbolically related, and their product, the RC time, is similar in patients with and without pulmonary hypertension and stable during treatment ; in CTEPH it measured 0.49 ± 0.2 s . Because of this hyperbolic relation, a modest rise in PVR from normal produces a disproportionately large fall in compliance, and the RV faces an increased pulsatile load early in the disease.

RV–PA coupling is conventionally reported as Ees/Ea, with a normal resting range of 1.5 to 2.0 . Under chronic pressure overload, the RV first adapts homeometrically, increasing contractility and wall thickness to raise Ees while chamber volumes are preserved. As afterload continues to rise, adaptation becomes heterometric, and the ventricle recruits the Frank–Starling mechanism through progressive dilation . In 42 patients with pulmonary hypertension, an Ees/Ea below 0.805 identified the onset of RV failure, defined as RV dilation with an EF below 35% . On MRI-derived pressure–volume loops, Emax/Ea was 1.1 ± 0.3 in six patients with chronic pulmonary hypertension and 1.9 ± 0.4 in six controls . The simulator contrasts the two stages directly. At a PVR of 7 WU, the hypertrophied RV of the compensated PAH scenario keeps Ees/Ea at 1.2 and cardiac output at 5.2 L/min, whereas the unadapted RV of the acute PE scenario falls to an Ees/Ea of 0.6 and a cardiac output of 3.9 L/min at a PVR of 6 WU.

Because the two ventricles operate in series, a fall in RV output directly lowers LV preload. In patients, this series effect is compounded by direct ventricular interdependence, in which RV dilation shifts the septum leftward and pericardial constraint limits LV filling; the model includes neither mechanism .

Model

P(V,t) = e(t)·Ees·(V − V₀) + [1 − e(t)]·A·(eβ(V − V₀) − 1)

The model comprises eight compartments, namely the two ventricles, the two atria, the systemic and pulmonary arteries, each represented as a three-element Windkessel, and the passive systemic and pulmonary veins. The ventricular activation function e(t) is a normalized double-Hill function . Each atrium is also a time-varying elastance whose contraction begins at the P wave, 160 ms before ventricular activation, and lasts 140 ms; this contraction generates the a wave and the late-diastolic atrial contribution to ventricular filling. The valves are ideal. The equations are integrated by a fourth-order Runge–Kutta method with 0.5-ms steps until the beats reach a steady state. Ea is measured from the simulated beat, using Pes at peak elastance, rather than set as an input. Model Pes is about 0.97 × SBP, so model Ea runs about 8% above the bedside estimate of 0.9 × SBP/SV.

Calibration and limits

The normal model has an EF of 58%, a blood pressure of 119/73 mmHg, a cardiac output of 5.7 L/min, a LAP of 7 mmHg, and an LV Ea/Ees of 0.62 . Its mPAP is 14 mmHg, compared with 14.0 ± 3.3 mmHg in healthy subjects , its PVR is 1.2 WU, and its RV Ees/Ea is 2.0. Atrial contraction supplies 17% of the stroke volume, and its loss, as in atrial fibrillation, lowers stroke volume by 9% in the normal heart and by 14% in the HFpEF scenario.

The model does not include ventricular interdependence, the pericardium, valve disease, the c wave, the effect of the descending base on atrial pressure, the baroreflex, the force–frequency relation, coronary flow, or the time constant of relaxation. Without reflexes, pressures in shock and in acute PE run lower than in patients. The HFpEF scenario also understates the lability of blood pressure seen in patients, in whom nitroprusside lowered pressure 2.6-fold more than in HFrEF .
