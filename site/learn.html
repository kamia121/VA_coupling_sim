Concepts

Ventricular contractility and arterial load can both be expressed as elastance, in mmHg/mL. Together with end-diastolic volume, their ratio determines stroke volume, ejection fraction, and the efficiency of ventricular work.

The pressure–volume loop

The loop is traversed counter-clockwise. Filling occurs along the bottom of the loop, isovolumic contraction along the right side, ejection along the top, and isovolumic relaxation along the left side. The width of the loop is the stroke volume, and its area is the stroke work.

End-systolic elastance (Ees)

Ventricular pressure at any moment of the cardiac cycle is the product of the elastance of the chamber at that moment and the volume it contains above an unstressed volume, V₀, so that P(t) = E(t) × (V − V₀). Elastance rises during systole and reaches its maximum at end-systole. This maximum is the end-systolic elastance, Ees .

Ees is the pressure the ventricle can sustain at end-systole for each milliliter of volume above V₀. A ventricle with a high Ees ejects to a small end-systolic volume against a high pressure, whereas a ventricle with a low Ees stops ejecting at a larger volume. End-systole corresponds to the upper left corner of the pressure–volume loop, where the aortic valve closes in the LV and the pulmonic valve closes in the RV. When preload or afterload is changed over a few beats, the end-systolic corners of successive loops fall on a straight line, the end-systolic pressure–volume relation (ESPVR), whose slope is Ees and whose volume intercept is V₀. The same relation holds for the RV .

Because the ESPVR is relatively independent of load, Ees is used as an index of contractility. EF, in contrast, falls when afterload rises even if contractility is unchanged. Ees increases with inotropes and sympathetic stimulation and decreases with ischemia, cardiomyopathy, and septic myocardial depression . Concentric hypertrophy also increases Ees , so a high Ees does not by itself indicate normal function. In the dilated, failing ventricle the ESPVR is flatter and shifted to the right, with a larger V₀. Diastolic stiffness is described separately by the curvilinear end-diastolic pressure–volume relation (EDPVR).

Effective arterial elastance (Ea)

Ea expresses the arterial load in the same units as Ees. It is defined as end-systolic pressure divided by stroke volume, Ea = Pes / SV , and represents the rise in end-systolic pressure for each milliliter ejected. Ea combines the resistance, compliance, and wave reflection of the arterial system in a single value. In patients, Ea measured from pressure–volume loops agreed with Ea calculated from a three-element Windkessel model of the arterial tree .

On the pressure–volume loop, Ea is the slope, without its sign, of a line drawn from end-diastolic volume on the volume axis to the end-systolic point, and a steeper line indicates a greater load. Ea is determined mainly by systemic vascular resistance divided by the cardiac period, Ea ≈ SVR / T. Ea correlated closely with SVR/T (r 0.97–0.99) and was less affected by pulsatile load . The dependence on heart rate reflects the shorter time available for arterial runoff at faster rates. Ea therefore increases with vasoconstriction, hypertension, and tachycardia and decreases with vasodilation and vasoplegia. At the bedside, Ea is estimated as 0.9 × systolic pressure / stroke volume .

The Ea/Ees ratio

The end-systolic point lies on both the ESPVR and the Ea line, and therefore at their intersection. For a given end-diastolic volume, the intersection determines end-systolic volume, end-systolic pressure, and stroke volume.

SV = (EDV − V₀) × Ees / (Ees + Ea)  ⇒  EF ≈ 1 / (1 + Ea/Ees)

The ventricle ejects the fraction Ees/(Ees + Ea) of the volume available above V₀. When Ees equals Ea, half of this volume is ejected; when Ees is twice Ea, two thirds are ejected; and when Ea is twice Ees, one third is ejected. EF follows almost directly from this fraction, which is why EF behaves as an index of coupling rather than of contractility. At the normal Ea/Ees of about 0.6, EF is about 60%.

A weak ventricle is more sensitive to afterload. A 50% increase in Ea reduces the ejected fraction by about 16% when Ees is 2.3 mmHg/mL and by about 24% when Ees is 0.8 mmHg/mL, and afterload reduction has a correspondingly larger effect in the failing heart. In the simulator, the formula predicts stroke volume within about 1 mL.

Derivation and assumptions

End-systole lies on the ESPVR, so Pes = Ees × (ESV − V₀). By the definition of Ea, the same point satisfies Pes = Ea × SV = Ea × (EDV − ESV). Equating the two expressions gives Ees × (ESV − V₀) = Ea × (EDV − ESV), and solving for ESV gives ESV = (Ea × EDV + Ees × V₀) / (Ees + Ea). Stroke volume is then SV = EDV − ESV = Ees × (EDV − V₀) / (Ees + Ea), and end-systolic pressure is Pes = Ea × SV = [Ees × Ea / (Ees + Ea)] × (EDV − V₀).

Dividing SV by EDV gives EF = [Ees / (Ees + Ea)] × (1 − V₀/EDV). When V₀ is small relative to EDV, EF ≈ 1/(1 + Ea/Ees). The same algebra gives SV/(ESV − V₀) = Ees/Ea, which is the basis for using SV/ESV as a volume estimate of Ees/Ea when V₀ is close to zero.

Ea approximates SVR/T because mean flow over one beat is SV/T. If the arterial load were purely resistive, mean pressure would be SVR × SV/T, and taking end-systolic pressure as approximately equal to mean pressure gives Pes/SV ≈ SVR/T. Compliance and wave reflection add modestly to this value .

The derivation assumes a linear ESPVR over the working range, a constant Ea from beat to beat, and a fixed EDV. In the intact circulation EDV depends on venous return and on the other ventricle, so a change in Ea also changes EDV. The simulator includes this interaction and the formula does not.

Stroke work is maximal when Ea equals Ees, and mechanical efficiency is maximal at a lower Ea . In isolated canine hearts, stroke work peaked at an Ea/Ees of 0.80 and efficiency at 0.70, and both remained within 90% of their maxima between 0.3 and 1.3 . Normal human hearts operate at an Ees/Ea of 1.62, or an Ea/Ees of about 0.62 , toward the efficient end of this range.

Disease with a normal ratio

When Ees and Ea change in the same direction, the ratio can remain normal while pressures, volumes, or flow are abnormal. In patients with HFpEF, Ees averaged 4.7 and Ea 2.6 mmHg/mL, a ratio of about 0.55 . The ratio should therefore be interpreted together with the absolute values of Ees and Ea, the filling pressure, and the cardiac output.

Stroke work and pressure–volume area

The pressure–volume area (PVA) is the sum of the stroke work and the potential energy, which is the area bounded by the ESPVR, the EDPVR, and the relaxation limb of the loop. In canine hearts, PVA was linearly related to myocardial oxygen consumption per beat , and the ratio SW/PVA is used as the mechanical efficiency. The simulator approximates the potential energy as ½ × Pes × (ESV − V₀).

The right ventricle

The RV has a thin wall and ejects into a low-resistance, high-compliance circulation. Its pressure–volume loop is trapezoidal or triangular, and ejection continues while pressure is falling. Pulmonary vascular resistance and compliance are inversely related; their product, the RC time, is similar across patients and does not change with treatment , and it measured 0.49 ± 0.2 s in CTEPH . A small increase in PVR from normal is therefore accompanied by a large fall in compliance.

RV–PA coupling is reported as Ees/Ea, with a normal value of 1.5 to 2 . In chronic pressure overload, the RV first adapts homeometrically, increasing Ees by hypertrophy while its volumes are preserved, and later heterometrically, by dilation . RV dilation and failure were associated with an Ees/Ea below 0.805 . On MRI, Emax/Ea was 1.1 ± 0.3 in pulmonary hypertension and 1.9 ± 0.4 in controls . Because the ventricles are in series, a low RV output reduces LV preload. In patients, septal shift and pericardial constraint add to this effect, and the model does not include them .

Model

P(V,t) = e(t)·Ees·(V − V₀) + [1 − e(t)]·A·(eβ(V − V₀) − 1)

The model consists of eight compartments, namely the two ventricles, the two atria, the systemic and pulmonary arteries, each represented by a three-element Windkessel, and the passive systemic and pulmonary veins. Ventricular activation follows a normalized double-Hill function . Each atrium is also represented by a time-varying elastance. Atrial contraction begins at the P wave, 160 ms before ventricular activation, lasts 140 ms, and produces the a wave and the late-diastolic filling of the ventricle. The valves are ideal. The equations are solved with a fourth-order Runge–Kutta method and a 0.5 ms step until the beat reaches steady state. Ea is calculated from the simulated beat, with Pes taken at peak elastance, and is not an input. Model Pes is about 0.97 × SBP, so model Ea is about 8% higher than the bedside estimate of 0.9 × SBP/SV.

Calibration and limits

The normal model has an EF of 58%, a blood pressure of 119/73 mmHg, a cardiac output of 5.7 L/min, a LAP of 7 mmHg, and an LV Ea/Ees of 0.62 . Its mPAP is 14 mmHg, compared with 14.0 ± 3.3 mmHg in healthy subjects ; its PVR is 1.2 WU, and its RV Ees/Ea is 2.0. Atrial systole supplies 17% of left ventricular filling. Removing atrial contraction, as in atrial fibrillation, reduces stroke volume by 9% in the normal heart and by 14% in the HFpEF scenario.

The model does not include ventricular interdependence, the pericardium, valve disease, the c wave or the effect of the descending base on atrial pressure, the baroreflex, the force–frequency relation, coronary flow, or the time constant of relaxation. Without reflexes, pressures in shock and in acute PE are lower than in patients. An R implementation of the same equations is available in the repository.
