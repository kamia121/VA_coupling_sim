Concepts

A pressure–volume loop shows how much blood a ventricle holds and how much pressure it generates during one beat. This chapter explains the loop before introducing ventricular elastance (Ees), effective arterial elastance (Ea), and their ratio. End-diastolic volume and the shape of the ventricle matter too. When you are ready to use the ideas, continue to Guided practice.

The pressure–volume loop

The loop runs counterclockwise. The ventricle fills along the diastolic limb at the bottom, contracts isovolumically up the right side, ejects across the top, and relaxes isovolumically down the left side. The width of the loop is the stroke volume, and its enclosed area is the external stroke work.

End-systolic elastance (Ees)

During systole, active contraction lets the ventricle generate more pressure at a given volume. A simple time-varying elastance model writes this relation as P(t) = E(t) × (V − V₀), where V₀ is the volume intercept. E(t), in mmHg/mL, rises through systole. Its end-systolic value is Ees . The model represents active pressure generation; a separate relation below describes passive filling.

Ees is the pressure the ventricle sustains at end-systole for each milliliter it contains above V₀. A ventricle with high Ees empties to a small end-systolic volume even against high afterload, whereas a ventricle with low Ees terminates ejection early at a much larger volume. On the pressure–volume loop, end-systole is the upper left corner, where the aortic valve closes in the LV and the pulmonic valve closes in the RV. When preload or afterload is altered over a few beats this corner moves, yet the end-systolic corners of successive loops remain on a single straight line, the end-systolic pressure–volume relation (ESPVR), whose slope is Ees and whose volume intercept is V₀. The RV obeys the same relation .

Because the ESPVR is relatively insensitive to acute loading conditions, Ees reflects the inotropic and geometric properties of the ventricle more reliably than ejection fraction does. EF declines with increasing afterload even when myocardial function is preserved. Ees, in contrast, increases with inotropic stimulation or sympathetic tone and decreases with ischemia, chronic cardiomyopathy, or septic myocardial depression . A steep ESPVR can also arise from concentric hypertrophy , so a high Ees does not necessarily indicate a healthy heart. At the other extreme, ventricular dilation yields a flattened, rightward-shifted relation with a larger volume intercept. Passive filling follows a separate curvilinear boundary, the end-diastolic pressure–volume relation (EDPVR), which describes diastolic stiffness independently of Ees.

Effective arterial elastance (Ea)

Effective arterial elastance, Ea, expresses arterial load in the same units as Ees. It is end-systolic pressure divided by stroke volume: Ea = Pes / SV . Ea was originally proposed as a single index of resistance, compliance, and wave reflections in the arterial tree . It does not measure the stiffness of one vessel, and it should not be used as a substitute for the pulsatile components of afterload.

On the pressure–volume loop, the Ea line runs from end-diastolic volume on the volume axis to the end-systolic point. It rises from right to left, so its slope is −Ea. A steeper line means that the ventricle ejects against a greater effective load. In humans, Ea tracks systemic vascular resistance and heart rate much more closely than arterial compliance or wave reflections: these two variables explained 96% to 98% of Ea's variability in a study of more than 2,500 adults . The rough relation is Ea ≈ SVR / T, where T is the duration of one beat. A shorter cycle leaves less time for arterial pressure to run off before the next beat.

At the bedside, Ea can be approximated as 0.9 × systolic blood pressure / stroke volume . That estimate needs a stroke-volume measurement; a higher MAP alone cannot tell you whether Ea, stroke volume, or both changed.

The Ea/Ees ratio

At the end of systole, the ventricle and the arterial system reach mechanical equilibrium. The end-systolic point must lie simultaneously on the ESPVR, which gives the pressure the ventricle can sustain at each volume, and on the Ea line, which gives the arterial pressure generated by ejecting each stroke volume. The only operating point that satisfies both constraints is the intersection of the two lines. This crossing sets the end-systolic volume and pressure and thereby fixes the stroke volume of the beat.

SV = (EDV − V₀) × Ees / (Ees + Ea)  ⇒  EF ≈ 1 / (1 + Ea/Ees)

The formula partitions EDV − V₀, the volume available to eject in this simple construction. When Ees equals Ea, half of that volume is ejected; when Ees is twice Ea, two thirds is ejected; and when Ea is twice Ees, one third is ejected. Ejection fraction (EF) also depends on V₀ relative to EDV, as the derivation below shows. EF therefore reflects ventricular properties and loading together; it cannot establish intrinsic contractility or healthy coupling by itself. With a resting Ea/Ees near 0.6 to 0.7 and a small V₀, EF is near 60%.

The failing ventricle is disproportionately sensitive to afterload for the same reason. With a normal Ees of 2.3 mmHg/mL, a 50% increase in Ea reduces the ejected fraction by about 16%; with an Ees of 0.8 mmHg/mL, as in a failing ventricle, the same increase reduces it by about 24%. By the same mechanism, afterload reduction yields a larger gain in stroke volume in a failing ventricle than in a normal one. In the simulator, the arterial vasodilator (SVR × 0.7) raises stroke volume by about 15% in the HFrEF scenario and by about 9% in the normal heart, and the formula itself predicts the simulated stroke volume to within about 1 mL.

Derivation and its assumptions

End-systole lies on the ESPVR, so Pes = Ees × (ESV − V₀). By the definition of Ea, the same point also satisfies Pes = Ea × SV = Ea × (EDV − ESV). Equating the two expressions gives Ees × (ESV − V₀) = Ea × (EDV − ESV), and solving for ESV gives ESV = (Ea × EDV + Ees × V₀) / (Ees + Ea). Stroke volume is then SV = EDV − ESV = Ees × (EDV − V₀) / (Ees + Ea), and end-systolic pressure is Pes = Ea × SV = [Ees × Ea / (Ees + Ea)] × (EDV − V₀).

Dividing SV by EDV gives EF = [Ees / (Ees + Ea)] × (1 − V₀/EDV). When V₀ is small relative to EDV, EF ≈ 1/(1 + Ea/Ees). The same algebra also gives SV/(ESV − V₀) = Ees/Ea, which is the basis for using SV/ESV as a volume estimate of Ees/Ea when V₀ is close to zero.

Over one beat, mean flow is SV/T. If the arterial load were purely resistive, mean pressure would be SVR × SV/T, and taking end-systolic pressure as approximately equal to mean pressure gives Pes/SV ≈ SVR/T. Compliance and wave reflection add only modestly to this value .

The derivation assumes that the ESPVR is linear over the working range, that Ea is constant from beat to beat, and that EDV is fixed. In the intact circulation, EDV depends on venous return and on the output of the other ventricle, so a change in Ea also shifts EDV; the simulator includes this interaction, whereas the formula does not.

In a simple analytical model at fixed preload, stroke work is maximal when Ea equals Ees, while oxygen-use efficiency peaks at a lower relative afterload . In ten isolated, blood-perfused canine hearts, stroke work peaked at an Ea/Ees of 0.80 and efficiency at 0.70. Both remained at least 90% of their maxima over a broad Ea/Ees range of 0.3 to 1.3 . That experimental near-optimal range is not a clinical normal range. In one study, the resting human LV had an Ees/Ea of 1.62, or an Ea/Ees of about 0.62 .

A similar ratio can hide different physiology

When Ees and Ea rise together, their ratio may stay close to its resting reference value while pressures and filling change substantially. In patients with HFpEF, mean Ees and Ea were 4.7 and 2.6 mmHg/mL, an Ea/Ees near 0.55 despite ventricular and arterial stiffening . Read the ratio alongside the absolute elastances, filling pressure, and cardiac output. In the simulator, HFpEF has an Ea/Ees of 0.51 and a left atrial pressure (LAP) of 16 mmHg. Adding 150 mL to the model's stressed blood volume raises LAP by 2.5 mmHg, versus 2.0 mmHg in the normal model. This button changes a model volume parameter; it does not represent a clinical infusion of exactly 150 mL.

Stroke work and pressure–volume area

The pressure–volume area (PVA) is external stroke work plus elastic potential energy stored at end-systole. In canine left ventricles, PVA correlated with myocardial oxygen consumption per beat (r = 0.89) . The quotient SW/PVA is the fraction of modeled mechanical energy converted into external work. It is not the same as efficiency measured against oxygen consumption, which includes an oxygen cost even when external work is small . The simulator approximates potential energy with the triangle ½ × Pes × (ESV − V₀), so its SW/PVA readout changes with Ees, Ea, and volume.

The right ventricle

Unlike the thick-walled left ventricle, the normal RV is a thin, crescentic chamber that ejects into a low-resistance, high-compliance vascular bed. Its pressure–volume loop is often trapezoidal or triangular, and ejection can continue after RV pressure starts to fall. Pulmonary resistance and compliance are inversely related, so an early rise in PVR may bring a substantial fall in compliance . Their product, the RC time, was nearly constant in cohorts with a normal pulmonary artery wedge pressure; elevated wedge pressure can lower compliance at a given PVR and shorten that time .

RV–PA coupling is conventionally reported as Ees/Ea, with a normal resting range of 1.5 to 2.0 . Under chronic pressure overload, the RV first adapts homeometrically, increasing contractility and wall thickness to raise Ees while chamber volumes are preserved. As afterload continues to rise, adaptation becomes heterometric, and the ventricle recruits the Frank–Starling mechanism through progressive dilation . In 42 patients with pulmonary hypertension, an Ees/Ea below 0.805 identified the onset of RV failure, defined as RV dilation with an EF below 35% . On MRI-derived pressure–volume loops, Emax/Ea was 1.1 ± 0.3 in six patients with chronic pulmonary hypertension and 1.9 ± 0.4 in six controls . The simulator contrasts the two stages directly. At a PVR of 7.3 WU, the hypertrophied RV of the compensated PAH scenario keeps Ees/Ea at 1.2 and cardiac output at 5.2 L/min, whereas the unadapted RV of the acute PE scenario falls to an Ees/Ea of 0.53 and a cardiac output of 4.3 L/min at a PVR of 7.7 WU.

Because the two ventricles operate in series, a fall in RV output directly lowers LV preload. This series effect is compounded by direct ventricular interdependence, in which RV dilation shifts the septum toward the LV and the pericardium limits the combined volume of the heart . In the acute PE scenario the septum shifts 13 mL toward the LV at end-diastole, and the Advanced page separates the septal, pericardial, and series effects.

Model

P(V,t) = e(t)·Ees·(V − V₀) + [1 − e(t)]·A·(eβ(V − V₀) − 1)

The model comprises eight compartments, namely the two ventricles, the two atria, the systemic and pulmonary arteries, each represented as a three-element Windkessel, and the passive systemic and pulmonary veins. The ventricular activation function e(t) rises as a normalized double-Hill function and decays exponentially with the relaxation time constant τ. Each atrium is also a time-varying elastance whose contraction begins at the P wave, 160 ms before ventricular activation, and lasts 140 ms; this contraction generates the a wave and the late-diastolic atrial contribution to ventricular filling. The valves are ideal unless a lesion is added. Seven further mechanisms are on by default, namely the pericardium, septal interdependence, the c wave and base descent, the relaxation time constant, the force–frequency relation, the baroreflex, and coronary perfusion; the Advanced page describes each and what it changes. The equations are integrated by a fourth-order Runge–Kutta method with 1-ms steps until the beats and the reflex reach a steady state, and the displayed beat is computed with 0.5-ms steps. Ea is measured from the simulated beat, using Pes at peak elastance, rather than set as an input. Model Pes is about 0.97 × SBP, so model Ea runs about 8% above the bedside estimate of 0.9 × SBP/SV.

Calibration and limits

The normal model has an EF of 57%, a blood pressure of 117/71 mmHg, a cardiac output of 5.6 L/min, a LAP of 7 mmHg, and an LV Ea/Ees of 0.62 . Its mPAP is 13 mmHg, compared with 14.0 ± 3.3 mmHg in healthy subjects , its PVR is 1.2 WU, its RV Ees/Ea is 2.0, and its fitted τ is 36 ms, compared with 35 ± 10 ms in controls . Atrial contraction supplies 17% of the stroke volume, and its loss, as in atrial fibrillation, lowers stroke volume by 11% in the normal heart and by 16% in the HFpEF scenario.

The model has no breathing, no coronary autoregulation below maximal flow, no pulmonary vasoreactivity, and no neurohormonal response beyond the baroreflex. The HFpEF scenario understates the lability of blood pressure seen in patients. With the baroreflex acting, the arterial vasodilator lowers MAP 1.7 times more in HFpEF than in HFrEF in the model, whereas nitroprusside lowered pressure 2.6-fold more in patients .
