Concepts

Ventricle and arterial load are both elastances (mmHg/mL). Their ratio sets stroke volume, ejection fraction and how energy is spent.

The pressure–volume loop

Counter-clockwise: filling along the bottom, isovolumic contraction up the right side, ejection across the top, isovolumic relaxation down the left side. Width = stroke volume. Area = stroke work.

Ees: how hard the ventricle can squeeze

Think of the ventricle in systole as a spring that stiffens as it contracts. At every instant, the pressure it generates is its stiffness at that moment times the volume it holds above an unstressed volume, V₀: P(t) = E(t) × (V − V₀). The stiffness E(t), called elastance (mmHg/mL), rises through systole and peaks at end-systole. That peak is Ees, the end-systolic elastance .

Ees has a practical meaning: it tells you how much pressure the ventricle can hold at the end of systole for each millilitre left inside it. A ventricle with a high Ees can finish ejection at a small volume even against a high pressure. A ventricle with a low Ees stops ejecting while it is still large. You can see this at the bedside by changing the load. If you change preload or afterload for a few beats, the end-systolic corners of the loops fall on one straight line, the end-systolic pressure–volume relation (ESPVR). Its slope is Ees and it crosses the volume axis at V₀. The RV behaves the same way .

Because the line barely moves when the load changes, Ees measures contractility in a way EF does not: EF falls when afterload rises even if the muscle is unchanged. Ees rises with inotropes and sympathetic drive and falls with ischaemia, cardiomyopathy or septic myocardial depression . Concentric hypertrophy also raises it: a thick, stiff ventricle has a steep ESPVR , so a high Ees is not always a sign of a healthy heart. A dilated failing ventricle often has both a flatter line and a line shifted to the right (a larger V₀). Filling is described by a separate, curved relation, the end-diastolic pressure–volume relation (EDPVR), which is diastolic stiffness and has nothing to do with Ees.

Ea: how hard the arteries push back

Ea, the effective arterial elastance, describes the load the ventricle ejects into, in the same units as Ees. It answers the question: for each millilitre the ventricle ejects, how much does end-systolic pressure rise? Ea = Pes / SV . It is not the stiffness of any single vessel. It folds the whole arterial tree (resistance, compliance and the reflections in large arteries) into one number, and in patients it matched an estimate built from a detailed model of the arterial load .

On the pressure–volume loop, Ea is a line that starts at end-diastolic volume on the volume axis (zero pressure) and rises to the end-systolic corner. Its slope is −Ea. A steep line means a heavy load. The largest single influence is resistance divided by heart period, Ea ≈ SVR / T. Ea tracked SVR/T closely (r 0.97–0.99), while pulsatile load mattered less . Heart rate appears because at a fast rate the arteries have less time to empty between beats, so each millilitre ejected leaves pressure higher. Ea therefore rises with vasoconstriction, hypertension and tachycardia, and falls with vasodilation and vasoplegia. At the bedside, Ea ≈ 0.9 × systolic pressure / stroke volume .

The ratio: where the two lines meet

At the end of systole, the ventricle and its load must agree. The end-systolic point has to lie on the ventricle's line (the ESPVR: the pressure it can hold at that volume) and on the artery's line (the Ea line: the pressure the arteries generate for that stroke volume). The only point on both lines is where they cross. That crossing sets end-systolic volume and pressure, and therefore stroke volume. Ees and Ea together decide how far the ventricle empties.

SV = (EDV − V₀) × Ees / (Ees + Ea)  ⇒  EF ≈ 1 / (1 + Ea/Ees)

Read the formula as a split. EDV − V₀ is the volume the ventricle could possibly eject. The ventricle ejects the share Ees/(Ees + Ea) and keeps the rest. When Ees equals Ea, it ejects half of that volume. When Ees is twice Ea, it ejects two thirds. When Ea is twice Ees, it ejects only one third. EF follows almost directly, which is why EF behaves like a coupling index rather than a measure of contractility. At the normal Ea/Ees of about 0.6, EF is about 60%.

The same share explains why a weak ventricle is so sensitive to afterload. A 50% rise in Ea cuts the ejected share by about 16% when Ees is 2.3 mmHg/mL (normal), but by about 24% when Ees is 0.8 (a failing heart). Afterload reduction helps a weak ventricle more for the same reason. In the simulator the formula predicts the model's stroke volume within about 1 mL.

Derivation, and what it assumes

End-systole lies on the ESPVR, so Pes = Ees × (ESV − V₀). By definition of Ea it also satisfies Pes = Ea × SV = Ea × (EDV − ESV). Setting the two equal gives Ees × (ESV − V₀) = Ea × (EDV − ESV). Solving for ESV gives ESV = (Ea × EDV + Ees × V₀) / (Ees + Ea). Then SV = EDV − ESV = Ees × (EDV − V₀) / (Ees + Ea), and Pes = Ea × SV = [Ees × Ea / (Ees + Ea)] × (EDV − V₀).

Dividing SV by EDV gives EF = [Ees / (Ees + Ea)] × (1 − V₀/EDV). When V₀ is small relative to EDV, EF ≈ 1/(1 + Ea/Ees). The same algebra also gives SV/(ESV − V₀) = Ees/Ea, which is the idea behind SV/ESV as a volume estimate of Ees/Ea when V₀ ≈ 0.

Why Ea ≈ SVR/T: over one beat, mean flow is SV/T. If the load were purely resistive, mean pressure would be SVR × SV/T. Taking end-systolic pressure as close to mean pressure gives Pes/SV ≈ SVR/T. Compliance and wave reflection add to this, but only modestly .

Assumptions: the ESPVR is linear over the working range, Ea is constant from beat to beat, and EDV is treated as given. In the body, EDV depends on venous return and on the other ventricle, so a change in Ea also shifts EDV. The simulator includes that, the formula does not.

Energy has its own optimum. Stroke work peaks at Ea = Ees, while efficiency peaks at a lower Ea . In isolated canine hearts, work peaked at Ea/Ees 0.80 and efficiency at 0.70, and both stayed ≥ 90% of optimum from 0.3 to 1.3 . Normal human hearts run at Ees/Ea 1.62 (Ea/Ees ≈ 0.62) , close to the efficient end.

In range, still sick

The ratio compresses two numbers into one. When Ees and Ea move together, the ratio stays in the normal band while pressures, volumes or flow are abnormal. HFpEF: Ees 4.7 and Ea 2.6 mmHg/mL, ratio ≈ 0.55 . The band markers stay on every screen; read them with the absolute Ees, Ea, filling pressure and output.

Stroke work and PVA

PVA = stroke work + potential energy (area between ESPVR, EDPVR and the relaxation limb). In canine hearts PVA is linear with myocardial oxygen consumption per beat . SW/PVA = mechanical efficiency. The simulator uses PE ≈ ½·Pes·(ESV − V₀).

The right ventricle

Thin wall, low-resistance high-compliance load, trapezoidal or triangular loop with ejection continuing as pressure falls. Pulmonary R and C are inversely related; their product (RC time) is similar across patients and stays constant with treatment ; 0.49 ± 0.2 s in CTEPH . A small PVR rise from normal brings a large compliance fall.

Reported as Ees/Ea: normal 1.5–2 . Chronic pressure overload: homeometric adaptation first (Ees up by hypertrophy, volumes preserved), then heterometric (dilatation) . Dilatation and failure below Ees/Ea 0.805 . MRI: Emax/Ea 1.1 ± 0.3 in PH vs 1.9 ± 0.4 in controls . Ventricles in series: low RV output lowers LV preload. Septal shift and pericardial constraint add to this in patients and are not modelled .

Model

P(V,t) = e(t)·Ees·(V − V₀) + [1 − e(t)]·A·(eβ(V − V₀) − 1)

Six compartments: two ventricles, systemic and pulmonary arteries (three-element Windkessels) and veins (passive, atria lumped in). e(t): normalized double-Hill activation . Ideal valves. RK4 at 0.5 ms to beat-to-beat steady state. Ea is measured from the beat (Pes at peak elastance), not set. Model Pes ≈ 0.97 × SBP, so model Ea runs about 8% above the bedside 0.9 × SBP/SV.

Calibration and limits

Normal: EF 57%, BP 116/71, CO 5.5 L/min, LAP 9, LV Ea/Ees 0.62 , mPAP 15 (healthy 14.0 ± 3.3 ), PVR 1 WU, RV Ees/Ea 2.0.

Not modelled: ventricular interdependence, pericardium, valve disease, atrial contraction, baroreflex, force–frequency relation, coronary flow, relaxation time constant.
