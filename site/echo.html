Echocardiographic assessment

Echo cannot measure a pressure–volume loop, but it can estimate the pieces: stroke volume, ventricular volumes, longitudinal function and, from Doppler, pressure. Each surrogate carries assumptions, and those assumptions matter most in the patients where coupling is most abnormal.

Left heart

Stroke volume

Doppler stroke volume is the product of the left ventricular outflow tract cross-sectional area and the velocity–time integral recorded by pulsed-wave Doppler at the same level . The area is calculated from the LVOT diameter assuming a circle, π(D/2)², so an error of 1 mm in a 20 mm diameter produces an error of about 10% in stroke volume. The VTI alone is a stroke distance in centimetres, not a stroke volume. It is useful for tracking change in the same patient, but it cannot be put into Ea = Pes/SV.

Arterial elastance

End-systolic pressure is not measured at the bedside. The common approximation is Pes ≈ 0.9 × brachial systolic pressure, which Chen and colleagues used in their non-invasive method and Guarracino and colleagues used in septic shock . Ea is then 0.9·SBP/SV . The 0.9 factor is itself an approximation. It is least reliable when the pressure waveform is abnormal (severe aortic stiffening, low-output states or vasopressor-dependent shock), and brachial pressure differs from central pressure.

End-systolic elastance

Chen and colleagues developed a single-beat method for Ees that uses cuff systolic and diastolic pressure, echo-Doppler stroke volume and ejection fraction, and an estimated normalized ventricular elastance . It was validated against invasive measurements over a wide range of Ees (0.4–8.4 mmHg/mL). The method is not reproduced here as a calculator. Readers who want to apply it should use the published equations directly.

Ejection fraction as a coupling index

If the ESPVR passes near the origin (V₀ ≈ 0), the algebra of the PV loop gives Ea/Ees ≈ ESV/SV = (1 − EF)/EF. An EF of 60% corresponds to Ea/Ees of about 0.67, an EF of 40% to about 1.5, and an EF of 25% to about 3. This is why EF behaves like a coupling ratio rather than a pure contractility index: it falls when afterload rises even if Ees is unchanged. The approximation fails when V₀ is large, as in dilated ventricles, and it cannot separate a high Ea from a low Ees.

Right heart

TAPSE/PASP

The ratio of tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion to Doppler-estimated pulmonary artery systolic pressure is the most widely used echo surrogate of RV–PA coupling. TAPSE stands in for RV contractile function (a length rather than an elastance) and PASP for load. TAPSE alone is prognostic: in pulmonary hypertension, TAPSE below 18 mm was associated with markedly lower survival . Guazzi and colleagues introduced it in heart failure, where a TAPSE/PASP below 0.36 mm/mmHg identified patients at higher risk . In pulmonary arterial hypertension, Tello and colleagues found that 0.31 mm/mmHg discriminated invasively measured RV–arterial uncoupling, defined as Ees/Ea below 0.805 . Their prognostic tertiles (< 0.19, 0.19–0.32 and > 0.32 mm/mmHg) are the thresholds used in the 2022 ESC/ERS risk assessment table .

PASP is usually estimated as 4·(peak TR velocity)² plus an estimate of right atrial pressure. The estimate depends on an adequate TR envelope, and in individual patients it can differ substantially from the catheter value. In 65 patients evaluated for pulmonary hypertension, Doppler PASP was more than 10 mmHg from the invasive value in 48%, and underestimates were larger than overestimates . TAPSE is angle-dependent and reflects longitudinal motion only, so it can underestimate function after cardiac surgery and be pulled along by LV motion. For these reasons TAPSE/PASP is a screening and risk tool. It is not a measurement of Ees/Ea.

Volume method: SV/ESV

When RV volumes can be measured, by cardiac MRI or three-dimensional echo, the same algebra as on the left gives Ees/Ea ≈ SV/ESV, assuming V₀ ≈ 0 . Vanderpool and colleagues found that an SV/ESV of 0.515 or less predicted worse outcome in patients referred for pulmonary hypertension . Because a dilated RV has a positive V₀, SV/ESV reads lower than the true Ees/Ea. In the simulator, compare the "true" Ees/Ea with SV/ESV in the decompensated PAH scenario to see the size of this bias.

Other pieces of the picture

RV size relative to the LV, septal flattening in systole (pressure load) or diastole (volume load), fractional area change, tissue Doppler S′, right atrial size and IVC behaviour all describe the RV side of the coupling equation . A notch in the RV outflow tract Doppler envelope indicates early wave reflection from a stiff, high-resistance pulmonary circulation. Arkles and colleagues found mid-systolic notching was 96% specific for PVR above 5 WU, and PA compliance was lowest in that group . None of these is a coupling ratio, but together they show whether the RV has adapted by hypertrophy or by dilatation.
