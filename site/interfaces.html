Hemodynamic interfaces in shock

Rola and colleagues describe the circulation in shock as four interfaces in series, each of which can uncouple: the left ventricle and the arteries, the arterioles and the capillaries, the capillaries and the venules, and the right ventricle and the pulmonary artery . Ventricular–arterial coupling, the subject of the rest of this site, is the first and the last of the four. The middle two carry blood from the arterial tree to the tissues and from the tissues back to the heart, and a patient can have a normal MAP and a normal cardiac output while one of them fails. That dissociation between the macrocirculation and the microcirculation is called a loss of hemodynamic coherence.

Every scenario named on this page can be opened in the Shock lab, where drugs and fluids are given on an accelerated clock and each interface is scored against the thresholds below.

I. Left ventricle and arteries

The first interface is the one described on the Concepts page. Stroke volume is set where the Ea line meets the ESPVR, and Ea/Ees summarizes the match between the ventricle and its load. In septic shock, uncoupling has been defined as an Ea/Ees above 1.36 . At the bedside, Ea is approximated as 0.9 × SBP/SV , and forward flow is read from the LVOT velocity–time integral, for which Rola and colleagues use 18 cm as the lower limit . Because EF equals SV/EDV and Ea/Ees is approximately (1 − EF)/EF when V₀ is small, EF itself behaves as a coupling index.

In the cardiogenic shock case of the Shock lab, Ea/Ees is 4.2 and the VTI is 9 cm. Dobutamine at 5 µg/kg/min lowers Ea/Ees to 2.8 within ten minutes, and norepinephrine at 0.1 µg/kg/min raises it to 4.9, because it raises Ea against a ventricle whose Ees barely changes.

II. Arterioles and capillaries

Arteriolar tone sets not only the resistance but also the pressure at which flow stops, the critical closing pressure (Pcc). Above Pcc, arteriolar flow depends on the difference between MAP and Pcc, not between MAP and venous pressure, so the difference MAP − Pcc is the pressure that drives tissue perfusion. In 5,988 admissions to a cardiac intensive care unit, this tissue perfusion pressure, estimated continuously from the arterial waveform, predicted mortality, length of stay, and peak lactate . The gap between Pcc and the mean systemic filling pressure is the vascular waterfall. In 30 vasoplegic patients after cardiac surgery, norepinephrine widened the waterfall in 19, and only in those patients did cardiac index, capillary refill time and the PCO₂ gap improve . On this view, a vasopressor restores perfusion only if it raises Pcc more than it raises Pmsf .

Capillary refill time is the bedside reading of this interface. In ANDROMEDA-SHOCK, resuscitation aimed at a normal capillary refill time and resuscitation aimed at lactate gave 28-day mortalities of 34.9% and 43.4% (hazard ratio 0.75, P = 0.06), with less organ dysfunction at 72 hours in the capillary refill arm . Capillary refill responds within minutes, whereas lactate falls over hours and rises for reasons other than hypoperfusion, among them β₂-adrenergic stimulation and impaired hepatic clearance.

The central venous–arterial PCO₂ difference reflects whether flow is adequate to wash out the CO₂ the tissues produce, and a gap above 6 mmHg identifies patients who remain inadequately resuscitated even when ScvO₂ exceeds 70% . In 85 patients with septic shock, a gap that remained at or above 6 mmHg after six hours of resuscitation was associated with more organ dysfunction and higher mortality at 28 days . ScvO₂ follows from the Fick principle, ScvO₂ ≈ SaO₂ − VO₂/(1.34 · Hb · CO · 10), so it falls when delivery falls relative to demand and is misleadingly high when the tissues cannot extract oxygen.

Delivery and consumption have a biphasic relation. Consumption is independent of delivery until extraction reaches a critical value, and below that point consumption falls with delivery and lactate rises. In critically ill patients whose life support was being withdrawn, the critical extraction ratio was 0.61 in septic and 0.59 in nonseptic patients, and the critical delivery was 3.8 and 4.5 mL/min/kg . The figure uses a critical extraction of 0.6, which with a demand of 3 mL/min/kg puts the critical delivery at 350 mL/min, or 5.0 mL/min/kg. At the normal cardiac output of 5.6 L/min, delivery is 882 mL/min, ScvO₂ 74% and the PCO₂ gap 4 mmHg; at 3 L/min, delivery is 476 mL/min, ScvO₂ 53% and the gap 7.4 mmHg.

In the vasoplegia case of the Shock lab, MAP is 64 mmHg while cardiac output is 9.4 L/min and ScvO₂ 79%, and the raised lactate comes from sepsis itself and not from a shortfall in delivery.

III. Capillaries and venules

Blood returns to the heart down the gradient between the mean systemic filling pressure (Pmsf), the pressure that the stressed volume of the systemic vessels exerts when flow stops, and the right atrial pressure. Venous return equals (Pmsf − RAP) divided by the resistance to venous return. Rola and colleagues give 7 to 10 mmHg as a normal Pmsf ; in ventilated patients after cardiac surgery, measured by inspiratory holds, it was 18.8 mmHg and rose to 29.1 mmHg after 0.5 L of colloid . The model's normal Pmsf is 7.8 mmHg, with a RAP of 4.1 mmHg and a resistance to venous return of 40 mmHg·s/L.

Adding 300 mL of stressed volume moves the venous return line to the right, raising Pmsf from 7.8 to 12.1 mmHg; the operating point moves up the normal cardiac function curve to a RAP of 8.1 mmHg and a cardiac output of 5.9 L/min. Removing 200 mL moves the line to the left, to a Pmsf of 5.3 mmHg, a RAP of 2.1 mmHg and a cardiac output of 4.9 L/min. The resistance to venous return is unchanged in both. With the same stressed volume, the failing LV of the cardiogenic case lets blood accumulate in the lungs, so the systemic Pmsf falls to 4.5 mmHg, and flow is 2.8 L/min at a RAP of 2.7 mmHg. A normal CVP in that patient describes the right heart and says nothing about the LA pressure of 23 mmHg.

Vasopressors act on both sides of the venous return gradient. Venoconstriction moves unstressed volume into the stressed pool and raises Pmsf, while arteriolar constriction raises the resistance to venous return. In 16 patients with septic shock, lowering norepinephrine from 0.30 to 0.19 µg/kg/min lowered Pmsf from 33 to 26 mmHg and cardiac index from 3.47 to 3.28 L/min/m² . After cardiac surgery, a small increase in norepinephrine raised Pmsf, SVR and the resistance to venous return in every patient, but cardiac output rose in only 6 of 16, those with a baseline stroke volume variation above 8.7% .

CVP is also the downstream pressure of every organ. When it rises, the perfusion pressure MAP − CVP falls, and the kidney, liver and gut become congested. The venous excess ultrasound score grades this congestion from the inferior vena cava and from hepatic, portal and intrarenal venous Doppler; after cardiac surgery, its severe grade was associated with acute kidney injury (hazard ratio 3.69) and predicted it better than CVP . Rola and colleagues regard a CVP above 10 to 12 mmHg as abnormal . Fluid moves this interface for only a short time. About 50% to 60% of an infused crystalloid remains in the vessels while it runs, and 15% to 20% half an hour after it stops , which the Shock lab reproduces with a faster loss when the capillaries leak.

IV. Right ventricle and pulmonary artery

The RV is coupled to the pulmonary circulation when its Ees/Ea is 1.5 to 2, and an Ees/Ea below 0.805 marks the onset of RV failure . A TAPSE/PASP below 0.31 predicted an Ees/Ea below 0.805 . When this interface fails, RAP rises, the venous return gradient narrows and the congestion of the third interface follows, so an uncoupled RV lowers flow at the first interface and raises backpressure at the third. The acute PE case shows both; there, norepinephrine restores RV perfusion and Ees , and fluid raises CVP without raising flow.

Flow against congestion

Forrester and colleagues classified patients after myocardial infarction by cardiac index and pulmonary artery wedge pressure (PAWP) into four subsets that guided therapy . Rola and colleagues plot the same two dimensions from ultrasound, forward flow as the LVOT VTI and congestion as the venous excess score or CVP, and follow the patient's path across the diagram toward adequate flow without congestion . The Shock lab draws this path with VTI against CVP.

What the model leaves out

The model has no microcirculation. Critical closing pressure, tissue perfusion pressure, capillary refill time and lactate are computed from the macrocirculation by illustrative functions, so the model can show why these markers move but cannot show a microcirculation that fails while every systemic variable is normal. ScvO₂ is treated as mixed venous saturation. The venous excess score cannot be simulated, and the Shock lab shows CVP in its place.
