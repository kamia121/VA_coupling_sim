Pressure lab

A pressure transducer reports the pressure at its own height, relative to the atmosphere. The number on the monitor depends on the vertical distance between the vessel and the transducer, the zero, the pressure around the vessel, and the dynamic response of the catheter and tubing.

Reference level and zero

Blood and the saline in the tubing both have weight. A column of saline 10 cm tall exerts 7.4 mmHg and a column of blood of the same height exerts 7.8 mmHg, so a pressure measured in a vessel depends on the height at which it is measured. The transducer reports the pressure at its own height, which is the pressure in the vessel plus the weight of the saline column between the vessel and the transducer. A transducer below the vessel adds the column to the reading, and a transducer above the vessel subtracts it.

The reference level is a convention. Intravascular pressures are interpreted at the level of the heart, the phlebostatic axis, so a transducer leveled to it reports a pressure as it would be at the heart wherever the vessel lies. In 196 patients, two commonly used zero levels differed by a median of 8.0 mmHg, and the choice of level changed the proportion classified as having pulmonary hypertension from 59% to 80% .

Zeroing opens the transducer to the atmosphere and sets its reading there to zero. It removes the offset and the drift of the transducer, but it does not correct the height, which is a separate step. A mounting leveled with the patient supine no longer matches the heart once the head of the bed is raised, because the heart rises above the transducer and the reading rises by the weight of the saline column between them.

The pressure at the heart is not the pressure at the brain. With the patient upright, the arterial pressure at the circle of Willis is lower than at the heart by the weight of the blood column, 19 mmHg for a vertical distance of 25 cm. Below the heart, the venous column is supported by the venous valves, so the pressure in a foot vein and in the capillaries of the foot rises with the height of the column. Perry and colleagues measured a foot venous pressure of 17.1 mmHg and a capillary pressure of 23.4 mmHg in recumbent subjects, and 95.2 and 112.8 mmHg on standing . Above the heart the veins collapse, and their pressure falls no lower than the atmosphere.

Pressure around the vessels

Positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) raises the pressure in the pleural space, and with it the pressure outside the heart and the intrathoracic vessels. A pressure measured against the atmosphere rises by the pleural pressure, while the transmural pressure, the distending pressure of the chamber, does not. Only part of the airway pressure reaches the pleural space. In patients with acute respiratory failure it was 24% when the total compliance was below 30 mL/cmH₂O and 37% when it was above 45 mL/cmH₂O , and in swine it was 62% in normal lungs and 34% in injured lungs . The extrathoracic veins stay at the atmosphere while the right atrium rises with the pleural pressure, so the gradient for venous return falls.

Catheter, tubing and transducer

A fluid-filled catheter, its tubing and the transducer form a second-order system. Its natural frequency is the frequency at which it rings after a sudden change in pressure, and its damping coefficient describes how fast the ringing dies away. The arterial pressure wave contains harmonics of the heart rate, and a harmonic near the natural frequency is amplified, so the systolic pressure is reported above its true value while the mean pressure is unchanged. In an in vitro system, air bubbles lowered the natural frequency from 17.89 to 7.35 Hz and raised the damping coefficient from 0.234 to 0.356 . Measurement systems with the damping coefficients found in subjects, below 0.4, were capable of systolic resonance artifacts of 8 mmHg or more, and compensation filters with damping coefficients of 0.6 to 0.7 removed them .

The fast-flush test measures both properties. After a brief flush the pressure falls to the arterial pressure and rings down. The period between two consecutive peaks gives the damped frequency, and the ratio of their amplitudes gives the damping coefficient through the logarithmic decrement. An overdamped system does not ring. It follows a step in pressure as an exponential with a time constant that grows with damping, so a sudden change reaches the monitor late and the pulse is rounded.
