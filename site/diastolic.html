Relaxation and chamber stiffness

Early diastole is active: myocardial relaxation drives an exponential fall in LV pressure (τ), generating a suction gradient that pulls blood from the left atrium. Once relaxation ceases, late diastolic pressure is governed passively by LV volume and passive chamber stiffness along the end-diastolic pressure–volume relation (EDPVR). Diastolic dysfunction typically begins with impaired relaxation before progressing to increased myocardial stiffness—two mechanically distinct processes tracked by different echocardiographic parameters .

Transmitral filling and pseudonormalization

Left ventricular filling is driven by the transmitral pressure gradient at valve opening. Early filling velocity (E) initially declines as active relaxation slows, but it rises again as progressive diastolic failure forces left atrial pressure upward. Conversely, late filling (A) reflects compensatory atrial contraction against ventricular compliance. Because transmitral flow couples relaxation rate with filling pressure, pulsed-wave Doppler alone cannot distinguish a normal filling pattern from a pseudonormal one—a poorly compliant ventricle filling under high atrial pressure produces the same E/A ratio as a healthy heart .

Resolving this ambiguity requires parameters that isolate relaxation from preload. Mitral annular tissue Doppler (e′) provides a relatively preload-independent surrogate for myocardial relaxation ; indexing inflow velocity to annular motion (E/e′) therefore correlates directly with elevated left atrial pressure . Deceleration time (DT) and isovolumic relaxation time (IVRT) likewise shorten as elevated atrial head pressure forces premature valve opening and rapid ventricular equilibration. Complementary indices capture downstream and upstream consequences: pulmonary venous flow shifts from systolic to diastolic predominance, the pulmonary vein atrial reversal wave (Ar) widens against a stiff ventricle, left atrial volume index (LAVI) integrates chronic pressure exposure, and tricuspid regurgitant velocity quantifies secondary pulmonary hypertension. Reflecting these hemodynamics, the 2016 ASE/EACVI guidelines use E/A and peak E as initial triage, corroborating elevated filling pressures through E/e′, peak TR velocity, and LAVI .

Progression through the grades

Grade I is impaired relaxation at a normal filling pressure. With a relaxation time constant nearly twice normal (68 against 36 ms), LV pressure is still falling when the mitral valve opens, so the early transmitral gradient narrows, E falls and the atrial kick carries a larger share of filling, reversing the E/A ratio. Mean LA pressure remains normal, while the prolonged IVRT and the reduced e′ reflect the relaxation abnormality itself.

Grade II is the pseudonormal stage. As chamber stiffness increases and volume is retained, mean LA pressure rises to about 16 mmHg and restores the early gradient that slow relaxation had narrowed, returning E/A to the normal range. The relatively preload-independent indices remain abnormal: e′ is still reduced, E/e′ is raised and the atrium has enlarged. Lowering LA pressure by removing volume uncovers the underlying relaxation pattern .

Restrictive filling marks grade III. A stiff ventricle fills from a stiff atrium at a mean pressure of about 21 mmHg; the opening gradient is large, but LV pressure climbs steeply once filling begins, truncating early inflow and shortening the deceleration time, and the atrial kick adds little against a ventricle that is already full. Preload reduction moves the ventricle down its EDPVR, and the pattern returns toward pseudonormal.

When the chamber is stiff enough that even a reduced volume sits on the steep limb of the EDPVR, the restrictive pattern persists after preload reduction; the older four-grade scheme called this fixed restrictive filling grade IV . Mean LA pressure approaches 30 mmHg, retrograde transmission of that pressure has raised mean pulmonary artery pressure to nearly 40 mmHg, and pulmonary venous inflow has become predominantly diastolic. The 2016 guideline does not separate this stage from grade III .

E/e′ loses sensitivity at high filling pressures. Once a stiff ventricle limits early inflow, further increases in LA pressure raise E only slightly, and the regression of Nagueh and colleagues (PAWP = 1.24 × E/e′ + 1.9) underestimates mean LA pressure by 2 mmHg in grade III and by 9 mmHg in grade IV . The low restrictive E velocity of the model accounts for part of the grade IV discrepancy.

Between patients, the grades overlap. Among 40 simulated patients per grade, 8 of the grade II patients have supporting criteria too mild to meet the guideline definition of raised filling pressure, and 17 of the grade III patients have an E/A ratio below 2. After 1.5 L is removed, 12 of the 40 grade III patients retain a restrictive pattern and 19 of the 40 grade IV patients lose it, so reversibility after preload reduction varies continuously between patients .

Clinical thresholds

Bedside consequences are defined by hemodynamic thresholds. Pulmonary congestion corresponds to a PAWP above 18 mmHg and low output to a cardiac index below 2.2 L/min/m² . The LA pressure at which alveolar edema develops depends on plasma protein concentration , and 25 mmHg, close to the normal plasma oncotic pressure, is taken as the edema threshold. Hypotension is a mean arterial pressure below 65 mmHg , systemic venous congestion a central venous pressure above 12 mmHg , and pulmonary hypertension a mean pulmonary artery pressure above 20 mmHg, post-capillary when PAWP exceeds 15 mmHg . Applied to pressures and flows alone, these thresholds leave lung water, renal function and symptoms outside the simulation.

Tolerance to volume, afterload and atrial fibrillation

Each grade of the virtual cohort contains 40 patients whose parameters are those of the grade, each multiplied by an individual log-normal factor with a coefficient of variation of 6% to 20%. The varied parameters are the relaxation time constant, the EDPVR, Ees, atrial function and volume, blood volume, SVR, arterial compliance, PVR and heart rate. Every patient undergoes the same protocols: intravascular volume change from −2 L to +2 L, SVR from 0.7 to 1.75 times baseline, a sympathetic surge, and AF at mean ventricular rates of 70 to 150/min. Charts and tables for each protocol are in the simulator window.

Volume loading

Added intravascular volume distributes to every cardiac chamber, and the EDPVR sets how much of the LV share appears as end-diastolic pressure. In the normal ventricle and in grade I, a 500 mL bolus still raises stroke volume and adds 0.1 to 0.2 L/min of cardiac output. From grade II onward, the ventricle operates on the plateau of its Frank–Starling relation and the steep limb of its EDPVR, and the same bolus raises mean LA pressure by 3 to 4 mmHg with no gain in output. A liter raises mean LA pressure by about 5 mmHg in the normal ventricle, close to the rise from 10 to 16 mmHg that Fujimoto and colleagues measured in healthy adults after about 1 L of saline. In their patients with HFpEF, PCWP rose by 25 mmHg per L/m², roughly 13 mmHg per liter , nearly twice the 7 to 8 mmHg of the late grades here, which consequently understate volume intolerance.

Volume removal

Diuresis runs the same relations in reverse. In grades III and IV, where the stiff ventricle gives up pressure faster than volume, removing 1 L lowers mean LA pressure by 8 to 9 mmHg while cardiac output falls by 0.1 L/min. Grade I, which relies on filling pressure and the atrial kick to reach its end-diastolic volume, loses more than 1.2 L/min with the same removal, a larger fall than that of the normal ventricle.

Volume window

The volume window, the range of volume change over which mean LA pressure stays below 18 mmHg while cardiac output stays within 10% of its plateau, narrows from 2.5 L in the normal ventricle to 1.4 L in grade II. In grades III and IV it lies entirely below baseline. The grade III patient must lose 440 mL to enter it, and the grade IV patient must lose about 1.2 L and then has only 370 mL of margin before underfilling; 3 of the 40 grade IV patients have no window.

Afterload

A rise in afterload enlarges LV end-systolic volume, and in a stiff ventricle the volume left at end-systole raises the next end-diastolic pressure. Taken alone, a 50% rise in SVR lowers stroke volume by 11% to 13% but raises mean LA pressure by less than 2 mmHg in every grade. Sympathetic venoconstriction during a hypertensive crisis also shifts unstressed venous blood into the central circulation; with 200 mL of such recruitment added, the rise in mean LA pressure grows with grade, reaching 6.4 mmHg in grade IV. Central redistribution of volume is the mechanism usually proposed for hypertensive pulmonary edema in HFpEF, and its magnitude here depends on the assumed recruitment.

Atrial fibrillation

Atrial fibrillation removes the atrial kick and, at rapid ventricular rates, shortens diastole. Loss of the kick alone, with the rate unchanged, lowers cardiac output by 10% to 14% in every grade. The response to rate depends on the grade. Grades I and II, whose filling depends on slow relaxation and on atrial contraction, lose 28% and 16% of their output at 130/min, and mean LA pressure rises by 6 to 8 mmHg as the shortened RR interval interrupts filling. In grades III and IV, the atrium contributes little and chamber stiffness fixes stroke volume, so cardiac output is maintained up to 110/min and falls by 6% to 8% at 130/min. These are acute effects. Patients with HFpEF and permanent AF are more congested, with more pulmonary vascular disease and a lower cardiac output than those without AF, which Reddy and colleagues attributed in part to atrial dilation and pericardial restraint ; atrial remodeling is not modeled.

Fluid followed by diuresis

A liter of crystalloid infused over 30 minutes raises mean LA pressure by 3 to 4 mmHg in grades III and IV without changing cardiac output. With 18% of crystalloid retained intravascularly and the remainder redistributed with a time constant of 10 minutes , only 194 mL of the liter remains in the circulation after an hour, and the rest has entered the interstitium, which in a patient includes the lung. Diuresis of 500 mL/h for 3 hours then lowers mean LA pressure to 11 mmHg in grade III and 17 mmHg in grade IV at a cost of about 0.2 L/min in cardiac output. Over the same period, grade III falls below the restrictive cutoff and grade II returns to a relaxation pattern, while grade IV remains restrictive.

Limitations of the model

The model has no long axis, so e′ is not simulated. It is scaled from the fitted relaxation time constant (e′ = 12 × 36/τ cm/s), because e′ follows relaxation and changes relatively little with preload . Opdahl and colleagues showed that e′ also depends on restoring forces and on LV pressure at mitral valve opening , and the model omits both effects. The tissue Doppler display takes the timing of each wave from the model and scales the e′ and a′ amplitudes in this way.

E responds less to LA pressure here than in patients. Because the mitral valve is modeled as an orifice without inertance, a rise in LA pressure changes E/A only modestly, and the restrictive E velocity is about 80 cm/s, lower than the 100 cm/s or more commonly recorded at the bedside. Showing reversibility therefore requires removal of 1.5 L, far more than the preload reduction of a Valsalva maneuver, and the grades are set mainly by the disease parameters.

Fluid that leaves the circulation is not tracked. Lung water, oxygenation, renal function and natriuresis are absent, and diuresis is specified as net intravascular removal after refill. The afterload protocol is run with the baroreflex open, and the 200 mL of venous recruitment in the sympathetic surge is an illustrative value. Atrial fibrillation is modeled as loss of atrial contraction with a fixed, irregular ventricular rate, without atrial remodeling, tachycardia-mediated dysfunction or rate-dependent changes in relaxation beyond those of the elastance model.

Cardiac index is calculated with a body surface area of 1.9 m². With this value, the grade III and IV reference patients have a resting cardiac index of about 1.8 L/min/m² and fall in the cold, wet subset before any intervention. Many patients with restrictive filling have a higher resting cardiac index than this. LAVI is the model's LA volume multiplied by 0.6, which gives the normal ventricle an index of 25 mL/m². All patients are synthetic, and none of the values is a treatment threshold.
