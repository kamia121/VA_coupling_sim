The grades on echo

Diastolic dysfunction is graded by the pattern of LV filling, which is set by two things that change at different rates: how fast the LV relaxes and how high the LA pressure has to be to fill it . In the normal heart of the model, most filling occurs early, driven by relaxation. E is 90 cm/s, A is 77 cm/s (E/A 1.17), the deceleration time is 226 ms, the lateral e′ is 11.8 cm/s and E/e′ is 7.6, with a mean LA pressure of 7 mmHg.

In grade I, relaxation is slow (fitted τ 68 ms against 36 ms), so the early gradient is small and the atrium does more of the work. E falls to 72 cm/s, A rises to 104 cm/s (E/A 0.69), IVRT lengthens from 92 to 173 ms and e′ falls to 6.4 cm/s. The LA pressure is still normal, at 8 mmHg. The E of this patient is above the 50 cm/s that the ASE/EACVI algorithm uses to call grade I without supporting criteria, and the algorithm reaches grade I here only because E/e′, TR velocity and LA volume are normal .

In grade II, relaxation is still slow but the LA pressure has risen to 16 mmHg, which restores the early gradient, so E/A returns to 1.39 and looks normal. The clues are those that do not depend on the gradient: e′ stays low at 5.9 cm/s, so E/e′ rises to 14.0, and the LA has enlarged (LA volume index 38 mL/m²). When 1.5 L is removed, LA pressure falls to 7 mmHg and E/A falls to 0.76, the impaired-relaxation pattern that the higher pressure had masked .

In grade III, the LV is stiff and fills from a stiff, high-pressure LA. E/A is 2.26 and the deceleration time is 141 ms, because LV pressure rises steeply after a small volume enters. LA pressure is 21 mmHg. Removing 1.5 L lowers E/A to 1.45, so the pattern is reversible. In grade IV, E/A is 5.35, the deceleration time is 125 ms, the pulmonary vein S/D ratio is below 1 (0.93), the TR velocity is 3.13 m/s, mean PA pressure is 39 mmHg and LA pressure is 29 mmHg. After 1.5 L is removed, E/A is still 2.10, which makes this the fixed restrictive pattern that the older four-grade scheme called grade IV . The 2016 algorithm has no grade IV and grades both patients as grade III .

The virtual cohort shows how well the algorithm separates the grades once patients vary. As found, it grades 32 of the 40 grade II patients as grade II and 8 as grade I. It grades 22 of the 40 grade III patients as grade III and 17 as grade II, because many of them sit near the E/A cutoff of 2. After 1.5 L is removed, 28 of the 40 grade III patients leave the restrictive pattern and 12 do not, and 19 of the 40 grade IV patients leave it and 21 do not. Reversibility is a continuum across patients, not a property of a grade .

E/e′ tracks LA pressure only loosely. With the regression of Nagueh and colleagues (PAWP = 1.24 × E/e′ + 1.9) , the estimate is 19 mmHg in grade III, where the model LA pressure is 21 mmHg, and 20 mmHg in grade IV, where it is 29 mmHg. Part of the gap in grade IV is the model's (see below), but the direction is the one to teach: once E is limited by a stiff ventricle, E/e′ stops rising with pressure.

Virtual cohort: fluid, afterload and AF tolerance

Each grade holds virtual patients. Each patient has the grade's parameters, each multiplied by its own log-normal factor (τ, EDPVR, Ees, atrial function and size, blood volume, SVR, arterial compliance, PVR and heart rate, with coefficients of variation of 6% to 20%). Every patient then goes through the same protocols: volume from 2 L removed to 2 L given, SVR from 0.7 to 1.75 times baseline, a sympathetic surge, and AF at mean ventricular rates of 70 to 150/min. Give fluid, then diurese

The reference patient of each grade receives two 500-mL crystalloid boluses over 30 minutes. From the first hour, diuresis removes 500 mL/h of intravascular volume for three hours. Crystalloid follows the Shock lab kinetics, in which 18% of the fluid stays in the vessels and the rest moves to the interstitium with a time constant of 10 minutes .

Teaching points

Fluid. In every grade, a liter of fluid raises LA pressure. The rise is 5.3 mmHg in the normal heart and 7.9 mmHg in grade IV. What changes across the grades is what the liter buys. Five hundred milliliters raise cardiac output by 0.11 L/min in the normal heart and 0.17 L/min in grade I. They raise it by 0.01 L/min in grade II, and in grades III and IV by nothing at all. From grade II onward, the ventricle is on the flat part of its output curve and the steep part of its pressure curve, so fluid adds congestion without flow. The normal rise matches the saline-loading study of Fujimoto and colleagues, in which wedge pressure rose from 10 to 16 mmHg after about 1 L in healthy adults. In the same study the rise in HFpEF was 25 mmHg per L/m², about 13 mmHg per liter at a body surface area of 1.9 m² . The model's late grades rise about half as steeply, so they understate the problem.

Diuresis. The mirror image holds. Removing 1 L lowers LA pressure by 7.7 mmHg in grade III and 9.5 mmHg in grade IV while cardiac output falls by only 0.1 L/min, so diuresis is the treatment that works for the late grades. Grade I is the exception worth remembering: it starts with a normal LA pressure, and the same liter removed lowers cardiac output by 1.27 L/min, more than in the normal heart (1.01 L/min), because a slowly relaxing ventricle depends on its filling pressure and its atrial kick.

The volume window narrows. Define the window as the range of volume over which LA pressure stays below 18 mmHg and cardiac output stays within 10% of its plateau. It is 2,510 mL wide in the normal heart (from 570 mL removed to 1,940 mL given), 2,050 mL in grade I and 1,440 mL in grade II. Grade III must lose between 440 and 1,500 mL to be in its window (1,060 mL wide), and grade IV must lose between 1,190 and 1,560 mL (370 mL wide). A grade IV patient has no fluid challenge that helps and a narrow margin between decongestion and underfilling. Three of the 40 virtual grade IV patients have no window at all.

Afterload. A 50% rise in SVR, with the baroreflex open, lowers stroke volume by 11% to 13% in every grade. It raises LA pressure by 0.4 mmHg in the normal heart and 1.6 mmHg in grade IV. Afterload alone is not what floods the lungs. When the same rise in SVR comes with the venoconstriction of a sympathetic surge, which moves 200 mL into the stressed volume, LA pressure rises by 3.6 mmHg in the normal heart, 4.9 mmHg in grade II and 6.4 mmHg in grade IV. The stiff ventricle turns a modest shift of volume into a large rise in pressure. This is the mechanism usually proposed for hypertensive pulmonary edema in these patients, and the model reproduces its direction, not its size.

Atrial fibrillation. At the same rate, losing the atrial kick lowers cardiac output by 10% to 14% in every grade. Rate is where the grades part company. At 130/min the normal heart keeps its output, and its LA pressure rises by 2.9 mmHg. Grade I loses 27% of its output and its LA pressure rises by 7.8 mmHg; grade II loses 15% and rises by 6.4 mmHg. Their filling depends on relaxation, which a short RR interval cuts off, and on the atrial kick. In grades III and IV the atrium was already contributing little and the stroke volume is fixed by the stiff ventricle, so a moderate rise in rate raises output: at 110/min, by 4% in grade III and 3% in grade IV. At 130/min both lose 4% to 5%, and LA pressure rises by 1.7 mmHg in grade III and falls in grade IV. These are acute hemodynamics only. Patients with HFpEF and permanent AF are more congested and have more pulmonary vascular disease, largely through the atrial remodeling that AF causes over years , and that chronic process is not in the model.

Fluid, then diuresis. In the time course, a liter of crystalloid over 30 minutes raises LA pressure from 21 to 24 mmHg in grade III and from 29 to 33 mmHg in grade IV, and cardiac output does not change. By 60 minutes only 194 mL of the liter remains in the vessels, and LA pressure has fallen back to 23 and 31 mmHg. The rest has gone to the interstitium, which in these patients includes the lungs. Diuresis at 500 mL/h then lowers LA pressure to 11 mmHg in grade III and 17 mmHg in grade IV by 4 hours, at a cost of about 0.2 L/min of output. The mitral pattern follows: grade III falls from E/A 2.26 to 1.70, below the restrictive cutoff, while grade IV falls from 5.35 to 2.74 and stays restrictive. In grade II, E/A falls from 1.39 to 0.85, back to a relaxation pattern. The normal and grade I patients have too little volume to spare for the full three hours, and their lines stop when the stressed volume reaches the model's floor.

What the lab leaves out

The model has no long axis, so e′ is not simulated. It is scaled from the fitted τ (e′ = 12 × 36/τ cm/s), because e′ tracks relaxation and changes little with preload . In reality, e′ also depends on restoring forces and on the LV pressure at mitral opening , and the model ignores both. The tissue Doppler display draws the timing of the waves from the model and sizes e′ and a′ this way.

The mitral E wave responds less to LA pressure in the model than in patients. The model has a mitral orifice but no inertance, so a rise in LA pressure shifts E/A only modestly, and a restrictive E stays near 80 cm/s instead of the 100 cm/s or more seen at the bedside. For the same reason, the reversibility test in the model needs 1.5 L removed, much more than a Valsalva maneuver removes. The grades are set mainly by the disease parameters, not by preload alone.

The late grades rise in LA pressure about half as steeply with fluid as patients with HFpEF do , so the model understates how poorly they tolerate volume. Crystalloid that leaves the vessels simply disappears: there is no lung water, no oxygenation, no kidney and no natriuresis, and diuresis is entered as net intravascular removal, with refill already subtracted. An afterload rise is run with the baroreflex open. The surge's 200 mL of venous recruitment is an illustrative size. AF is modeled as the loss of atrial contraction with a fixed, irregular ventricular rate. When a beat follows a short one, the model's AV-plane term gives a brief jump in mitral flow at the QRS, and the Doppler display holds the first 40 ms of such a beat to the flow before it. There is no atrial remodeling, no tachycardia-mediated dysfunction and no rate-dependent change in relaxation beyond what the elastance model gives. The LA volume index is the model's LA volume scaled by 0.6, so that the normal heart reads 25 mL/m². Every patient is synthetic, and none of the numbers here is a treatment threshold.
