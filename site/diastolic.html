Relaxation and chamber stiffness

Early diastole is active: myocardial relaxation drives an exponential fall in LV pressure (τ), generating a suction gradient that pulls blood from the left atrium. Once relaxation ceases, late diastolic pressure is governed passively by LV volume and passive chamber stiffness along the end-diastolic pressure–volume relation (EDPVR). Diastolic dysfunction typically begins with impaired relaxation before progressing to increased myocardial stiffness—two mechanically distinct processes tracked by different echocardiographic parameters .

Transmitral filling and pseudonormalization

Left ventricular filling is driven by the transmitral pressure gradient at valve opening. Early filling velocity (E) initially declines as active relaxation slows, but it rises again as progressive diastolic failure forces left atrial pressure upward. Conversely, late filling (A) reflects compensatory atrial contraction against ventricular compliance. Because transmitral flow couples relaxation rate with filling pressure, pulsed-wave Doppler alone cannot distinguish a normal filling pattern from a pseudonormal one—a poorly compliant ventricle filling under high atrial pressure produces the same E/A ratio as a healthy heart .

Resolving this ambiguity requires parameters that isolate relaxation from preload. Mitral annular tissue Doppler (e′) provides a relatively preload-independent surrogate for myocardial relaxation ; indexing inflow velocity to annular motion (E/e′) therefore correlates directly with elevated left atrial pressure . Deceleration time (DT) and isovolumic relaxation time (IVRT) likewise shorten as elevated atrial head pressure forces premature valve opening and rapid ventricular equilibration. Complementary indices capture downstream and upstream consequences: pulmonary venous flow shifts from systolic to diastolic predominance, the pulmonary vein atrial reversal wave (Ar) widens against a stiff ventricle, left atrial volume index (LAVI) integrates chronic pressure exposure, and tricuspid regurgitant velocity quantifies secondary pulmonary hypertension. Reflecting these hemodynamics, the 2016 ASE/EACVI guidelines use E/A and peak E as initial triage, corroborating elevated filling pressures through E/e′, peak TR velocity, and LAVI .

Progression through the grades

The grades describe one continuum. Relaxation slows first, chamber stiffness increases later, and mean LA pressure rises at each step to preserve filling. Because each stage is defined by how far LA pressure has compensated, the mitral inflow pattern changes direction twice before it stabilizes: E/A falls, returns to the normal range and then exceeds it. Representative values for each grade are listed in the grade table.

In the earliest stage, relaxation is prolonged while chamber stiffness and filling pressure remain normal. LV pressure is still declining when the mitral valve opens, so the early transmitral gradient is small and early filling is slow and incomplete, leaving a larger share of filling to the atrial kick. The mitral pattern reverses, with E smaller than A, the deceleration time and isovolumic relaxation time lengthen, and e′ falls with the relaxation defect. With LA pressure still normal, filling at rest is preserved, but it depends on the atrial kick and on the duration of diastole.

As chamber stiffness increases and sodium and water are retained, mean LA pressure rises. The higher atrial pressure restores the early transmitral gradient that slow relaxation had narrowed, and E/A returns to the normal range although neither lesion has improved. This pseudonormal stage, grade II, is recognized only through indices that do not depend on the transmitral gradient: e′ remains low, E/e′ rises with filling pressure, and the atrium dilates under sustained pressure load. Reducing preload, by a Valsalva maneuver or by diuresis, lowers LA pressure and uncovers the impaired relaxation pattern beneath .

With further stiffening, the ventricle accepts volume only at high pressure, and the restrictive pattern of grade III appears. The transmitral gradient at valve opening is large and early filling is rapid, yet LV pressure rises so steeply as the first volume enters that it soon equals atrial pressure, producing a tall E wave with a short deceleration time. Contracting into a ventricle that is already full, the atrium moves little additional blood forward and ejects part of its volume backward into the pulmonary veins, so the A wave is small and the pulmonary venous atrial reversal is prominent. The pattern is still load-dependent at this stage: lowering filling pressure moves the ventricle to a flatter part of its EDPVR, and the inflow pattern returns toward pseudonormal.

Once chamber stiffness is high enough that even a reduced volume lies on the steep limb of the EDPVR, the restrictive pattern persists after preload reduction. The older four-grade scheme designated this fixed restrictive filling as grade IV , a stage the 2016 guideline includes in grade III . Chronically raised LA pressure is transmitted retrogradely to the pulmonary circulation and produces post-capillary pulmonary hypertension, and pulmonary venous inflow becomes predominantly diastolic because the atrium, already distended, accepts little inflow during ventricular systole.

The relation between E/e′ and filling pressure also changes along the continuum. Through the middle grades, E rises with LA pressure while e′ stays low, and E/e′ follows filling pressure . In the restrictive grades of the simulation, E rises less steeply with further increases in LA pressure, so that E/e′ underestimates the highest filling pressures; the simulated restrictive E velocity is lower than values commonly recorded in patients, which exaggerates this effect.

The boundaries between grades are not sharp. Individual differences in relaxation, chamber stiffness, atrial function and volume status spread each grade into its neighbors, so the echocardiographic classification of a patient near a boundary can differ from the underlying stage, and reversibility after preload reduction varies continuously between patients . Clinical thresholds

Bedside consequences are defined by hemodynamic thresholds. Pulmonary congestion corresponds to a PAWP above 18 mmHg and low output to a cardiac index below 2.2 L/min/m² . The LA pressure at which alveolar edema develops depends on plasma protein concentration , and 25 mmHg, close to the normal plasma oncotic pressure, is taken as the edema threshold. Hypotension is a mean arterial pressure below 65 mmHg , systemic venous congestion a central venous pressure above 12 mmHg , and pulmonary hypertension a mean pulmonary artery pressure above 20 mmHg, post-capillary when PAWP exceeds 15 mmHg . Applied to pressures and flows alone, these thresholds leave lung water, renal function and symptoms outside the simulation.

Tolerance to volume, afterload and atrial fibrillation

Each grade of the virtual cohort contains 40 patients whose parameters are those of the grade, each multiplied by an individual log-normal factor with a coefficient of variation of 6% to 20%. The varied parameters are the relaxation time constant, the EDPVR, Ees, atrial function and volume, blood volume, SVR, arterial compliance, PVR and heart rate. Every patient undergoes the same protocols: intravascular volume change from −2 L to +2 L, SVR from 0.7 to 1.75 times baseline, a sympathetic surge, and AF at mean ventricular rates of 70 to 150/min. Charts and tables for each protocol are in the simulator window.

Volume loading

Added intravascular volume distributes to every cardiac chamber, and the EDPVR sets how much of the LV share appears as end-diastolic pressure. In the normal ventricle and in grade I, a 500 mL bolus still raises stroke volume and adds 0.1 to 0.2 L/min of cardiac output. From grade II onward, the ventricle operates on the plateau of its Frank–Starling relation and the steep limb of its EDPVR, and the same bolus raises mean LA pressure by 3 to 4 mmHg with no gain in output. A liter raises mean LA pressure by about 5 mmHg in the normal ventricle, close to the rise from 10 to 16 mmHg that Fujimoto and colleagues measured in healthy adults after about 1 L of saline. In their patients with HFpEF, PCWP rose by 25 mmHg per L/m², roughly 13 mmHg per liter , nearly twice the 7 to 8 mmHg of the late grades here, which consequently understate volume intolerance.

Volume removal

Diuresis runs the same relations in reverse. In grades III and IV, where the stiff ventricle gives up pressure faster than volume, removing 1 L lowers mean LA pressure by 8 to 9 mmHg while cardiac output falls by 0.1 L/min. Grade I, which relies on filling pressure and the atrial kick to reach its end-diastolic volume, loses more than 1.2 L/min with the same removal, a larger fall than that of the normal ventricle.

Volume window

The volume window, the range of volume change over which mean LA pressure stays below 18 mmHg while cardiac output stays within 10% of its plateau, narrows from 2.5 L in the normal ventricle to 1.45 L in grade II. In grades III and IV it lies entirely below baseline. The grade III patient must lose 440 mL to enter it, and the grade IV patient must lose about 1.2 L and then has only 370 mL of margin before underfilling; 3 of the 40 grade IV patients have no window.

Afterload

A rise in afterload enlarges LV end-systolic volume, and in a stiff ventricle the volume left at end-systole raises the next end-diastolic pressure. Taken alone, a 50% rise in SVR lowers stroke volume by 11% to 13% but raises mean LA pressure by less than 2 mmHg in every grade. Sympathetic venoconstriction during a hypertensive crisis also shifts unstressed venous blood into the central circulation; with 200 mL of such recruitment added, the rise in mean LA pressure grows with grade, reaching 6.4 mmHg in grade IV. Central redistribution of volume is the mechanism usually proposed for hypertensive pulmonary edema in HFpEF, and its magnitude here depends on the assumed recruitment.

Liberation from mechanical ventilation

Positive-pressure ventilation raises pleural pressure, which impedes venous return and lowers LV transmural afterload, and a spontaneous breathing trial withdraws both effects while sympathetic activation raises the heart rate. In patients with severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and cardiovascular disease, mean esophageal pressure fell from 5 to −2 mmHg when the ventilator was withdrawn, and 10 minutes of spontaneous breathing raised transmural pulmonary artery occlusion pressure (PAOP) from 8 to 25 mmHg with an unchanged LV ejection fraction; after diuresis, 9 of the 15 patients were weaned . The ventilator in the simulator raises mean pleural pressure by 7 mmHg. The breathing trial lowers it to 4 mmHg below quiet breathing, standing for labored inspiration, raises the sinus rate to 85/min, moves 200 mL into the stressed volume by venoconstriction and lowers SVR by 15%. Intrathoracic pressures are reported transmural. Between the two states, transmural LA pressure rises from 5 to 14 mmHg in the grade I patient and from 11 to 23 mmHg in the grade II patient, whose stiffer EDPVR converts the returning volume into a larger rise in filling pressure. During ventilation, the ASE algorithm classifies both patients as grade I.

A grade I pattern recorded during ventilation describes the filling pressure under the loading conditions of that moment. During the trial, E/A in the grade I patient stays below 0.8. At 85/min the atrium contracts while early inflow is still running, and that residual flow adds to A, so the A wave grows with the E wave. E/e′ rises with mean LA pressure, because e′, a relatively preload-independent surrogate of relaxation, does not share the rise in E. In 39 ventilated patients who had failed two breathing trials, an E/A above 0.95 combined with an E/e′ above 8.5 at the end of the trial identified a PAOP of 18 mmHg or more with a sensitivity of 82% and a specificity of 91% .

Myocardial ischemia during the trial adds to the load. Coronary microembolization in dogs lengthened τ from 31 to 49 ms and lowered ejection fraction . With τ lengthened 1.6-fold and Ees lowered by 15%, the grade II patient reaches 25 mmHg during the trial, and early and atrial inflow merge into a single wave.

Atrial fibrillation

Atrial fibrillation removes the atrial kick and, at rapid ventricular rates, shortens diastole. Loss of the kick alone, with the rate unchanged, lowers cardiac output by 10% to 14% in every grade. The response to rate depends on the grade. Grades I and II, whose filling depends on slow relaxation and on atrial contraction, lose 28% and 17% of their output at 130/min, and mean LA pressure rises by 6 to 8 mmHg as the shortened RR interval interrupts filling. In grades III and IV, the atrium contributes little and chamber stiffness fixes stroke volume, so cardiac output is maintained up to 110/min and falls by 6% to 8% at 130/min. These are acute effects. Patients with HFpEF and permanent AF are more congested, with more pulmonary vascular disease and a lower cardiac output than those without AF, which Reddy and colleagues attributed in part to atrial dilation and pericardial restraint ; atrial remodeling is not modeled.

Fluid followed by diuresis

A liter of crystalloid infused over 30 minutes raises mean LA pressure by 3 to 4 mmHg in grades III and IV without changing cardiac output. With 18% of crystalloid retained intravascularly and the remainder redistributed with a time constant of 10 minutes , only 194 mL of the liter remains in the circulation after an hour, and the rest has entered the interstitium, which in a patient includes the lung. Diuresis of 500 mL/h for 3 hours then lowers mean LA pressure to 11 mmHg in grade III and 17 mmHg in grade IV at a cost of about 0.2 L/min in cardiac output. Over the same period, grade III falls below the restrictive cutoff and grade II returns to a relaxation pattern, while grade IV remains restrictive.

Limitations of the model

The model has no long axis, so e′ is not simulated. It is scaled from the fitted relaxation time constant (e′ = 12 × 36/τ cm/s), because e′ follows relaxation and changes relatively little with preload . Opdahl and colleagues showed that e′ also depends on restoring forces and on LV pressure at mitral valve opening , and the model omits both effects. The tissue Doppler display takes the timing of each wave from the model and scales the e′ and a′ amplitudes in this way.

E responds less to LA pressure here than in patients. Because the mitral valve is modeled as an orifice without inertance, a rise in LA pressure changes E/A only modestly, and the restrictive E velocity is about 80 cm/s, lower than the 100 cm/s or more commonly recorded at the bedside. Showing reversibility therefore requires removal of 1.5 L, far more than the preload reduction of a Valsalva maneuver, and the grades are set mainly by the disease parameters.

Atrial contraction has a length–tension plateau and a force–velocity limit, the latter an internal resistance proportional to developed pressure, as described for the LV by Shroff and colleagues . Without these limits, developed pressure and emptying rate rise in proportion to atrial stretch, and A reaches 164 cm/s in the grade I patient during the breathing trial. In patients, active atrial stroke volume rises with LA volume, and active emptying fraction declines in the most dilated atria ; the model represents that decline through the lower atrial elastance of grades III and IV. The parameters of both limits were chosen to reproduce A velocities and were not measured.

Fluid that leaves the circulation is not tracked. Lung water, oxygenation, renal function and natriuresis are absent, and diuresis is specified as net intravascular removal after refill. The afterload protocol is run with the baroreflex open, and the 200 mL of venous recruitment in the sympathetic surge is an illustrative value. Pleural pressure is a mean value without a respiratory cycle, so the inspiratory swings of a labored breathing trial are represented only by their mean, and the value of −4 mmHg is illustrative. Ischemia is applied to the whole ventricle, and its fall in Ees is an illustrative value. Atrial fibrillation is modeled as loss of atrial contraction with a fixed, irregular ventricular rate, without atrial remodeling, tachycardia-mediated dysfunction or rate-dependent changes in relaxation beyond those of the elastance model.

Cardiac index is calculated with a body surface area of 1.9 m². With this value, the grade III and IV reference patients have a resting cardiac index of about 1.8 L/min/m² and fall in the cold, wet subset before any intervention. Many patients with restrictive filling have a higher resting cardiac index than this. LAVI is the model's LA volume multiplied by 0.6, which gives the normal ventricle an index of 24 mL/m². All patients are synthetic, and none of the values is a treatment threshold.
