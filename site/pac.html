Pulmonary artery catheter

Right heart catheterization measures pressures and flow directly. From them you can describe the pulmonary arterial load in detail and, with some assumptions, estimate how well the RV is coupled to it.

What is measured

A standard study records right atrial pressure (RAP), RV pressure during passage, pulmonary artery systolic, diastolic and mean pressures (PASP, PADP, mPAP), pulmonary artery wedge pressure (PAWP) as a surrogate of left atrial pressure, and cardiac output by thermodilution or the direct Fick method. Stroke volume is cardiac output divided by heart rate. All pressures are read at end-expiration with the transducer zeroed at the level of the left atrium.

The 2022 ESC/ERS guidelines define pulmonary hypertension as mPAP above 20 mmHg at rest. Pre-capillary PH is defined by PAWP of 15 mmHg or less with PVR above 2 WU. Isolated post-capillary PH is PAWP above 15 mmHg with PVR of 2 WU or less, and combined post- and pre-capillary PH is PAWP above 15 mmHg with PVR above 2 WU . The 20 mmHg threshold was introduced by the 6th World Symposium task force, which kept a PVR threshold of 3 WU at the time . The diastolic pressure gradient was proposed as a marker of pulmonary vascular disease that is less affected by flow and left atrial pressure than the TPG . Resting mPAP in healthy subjects is 14.0 ± 3.3 mmHg .

Resistance and compliance are not independent in the lung. Their product, the RC time, is roughly constant across patients and during treatment , and in chronic thromboembolic disease it measured 0.49 ± 0.2 s . PAC therefore falls steeply as PVR rises from normal, and a lower PVR from therapy brings a disproportionate gain in compliance. PA compliance measured as SV/PP is an approximation that treats the pulmonary circulation as a single compliance .

In acute inferior myocardial infarction, a PAPi of 0.9 or less identified severe RV dysfunction with high sensitivity and specificity . PAPi has since been used more widely in cardiogenic shock and advanced heart failure. It is calculated as PA pulse pressure divided by RAP . Thresholds in those settings come from different cohorts, and it should not be transferred between contexts without checking the source.

Estimating RV–PA coupling invasively

The reference method is the multi-beat approach: RV pressure and volume are recorded with a conductance catheter while preload is reduced (for example by transient caval occlusion), the ESPVR is fitted to the end-systolic points, and its slope is Ees. This is a research procedure.

The single-beat method avoids caval occlusion. A sine wave is fitted to the isovolumic portions of a single high-fidelity RV pressure curve to estimate the maximal pressure the RV would generate if it contracted without ejecting (Pmax). Brimioulle and colleagues showed that the line from Pmax at end-diastolic volume, tangent to the end-systolic point, estimates the ESPVR . Because the same triangle defines both elastances, the coupling ratio simplifies to

Ees/Ea ≈ Pmax / Pes − 1

where mean PA pressure is often used for Pes . The method needs a good RV pressure waveform. A fluid-filled pulmonary artery catheter can record RV pressure during insertion, but damping and catheter whip affect the isovolumic upstroke that the sine-wave fit depends on. Treat bedside estimates as approximate.

Effective PA elastance on its own, Ea ≈ mPAP/SV or PASP/SV, is a useful single number for RV afterload. It rises with PVR, with lower compliance and with heart rate.

The left side, invasively

With an arterial line and a cardiac output, LV Ea can be approximated as 0.9·SBP/SV . Ees cannot be measured from a PA catheter. Mixed venous saturation, lactate and urine output tell you whether the coupled system is delivering enough flow. That is a different question from whether it is coupled efficiently, and septic vasoplegia is the clearest example of the two diverging .
