Pulmonary artery catheter

Right heart catheterization measures intracardiac and pulmonary pressures and cardiac output directly. These measurements describe the pulmonary load and, with some assumptions, allow RV–PA coupling to be estimated.

Float the catheter

The tracing is the pressure at the catheter tip, generated from the model beat of the selected patient. The tip can be advanced from the RA through the RV and PA to the wedge position, where a thin lavender line shows the true left atrial pressure, or all four positions can be shown at once, stacked over one ECG. The diagram beside the tracing shows where the tip sits. Dotted lines mark where each pressure is read and where that point falls on the ECG, and the vertical scale fits the site on screen unless a fixed range is chosen. Up to 24 s can be shown on one screen, and playback can be slowed, so breathing and the challenges below can be followed over time. The atrial rhythm and valve lesion controls change the a, c, x, v, and y waves, and the artifact controls show the difference between the monitor reading and the true pressure, which is drawn as a faint gray line.

Waveforms by position

RA pressure is low and is reported as a mean. The RV systolic peak equals the PA systolic pressure, and RV diastolic pressure is close to RA pressure, so RV pressure is reported as systolic over end-diastolic. PA diastolic pressure stays well above RV diastolic pressure because the pulmonic valve closes. In the wedge position the balloon occludes a branch of the PA, and the tip senses pulmonary venous (left atrial) pressure through the capillary bed, so the PAWP tracing is lower and smoother than the PA wave.

Damping

An overdamped system, caused by an air bubble, a clot, or kinked or compliant tubing, gives a rounded trace in which the systolic pressure reads low. An underdamped system, caused by resonance or catheter whip, gives overshoot and ringing, and the systolic pressure reads high. Mean pressure is relatively insensitive to damping . The fast-flush test checks damping at the bedside .

Transducer height

Every pressure shifts with the height of the hydrostatic column, and a leveling error of 10 cm changes the readings by about 7.5 mmHg . In 196 patients, two commonly used zero levels differed by a median of 8.0 mmHg, and the choice of level changed the proportion classified as having PH from 59% to 80% . A transducer below the reference level reads high, and one above it reads low.

Where to read

The RA pressure is read at the base of the c wave at end-expiration, over three breaths; this is the pressure at tricuspid closure, and so the RV end-diastolic pressure. Where there is no c wave, the mean of the a wave or the pressure at the end of the QRS is used instead . In ventilated, paralyzed patients, mean CVP and end-diastolic CVP at end-expiration differed by 0.58 ± 0.81 mmHg . RVEDP and PADP are read at end-diastole, just before the systolic upstroke, and PAWP as the mean of the a wave . With the dotted lines on, a vertical line marks the reading point on the beat nearest end-expiration and carries it down to the ECG, and a horizontal line marks the value. At the wedge, the a wave falls just after the QRS, because LA pressure reaches the tip through the capillary bed, 60 ms later in the model.

Fluid challenge

In patients with pulmonary hypertension and a normal or high-normal PAWP, a PAWP above 18 mmHg after a rapid infusion of 500 mL of saline points to left heart disease . The rise depends on the baseline: in 174 patients, PAWP rose by 6.9 ± 3.6 mmHg, and 81% of those with a baseline PAWP of 13 to 15 mmHg exceeded 18 mmHg, so the test separates little in that range . Outside the catheter laboratory, a CVP above 10 mmHg makes a rise in cardiac output with volume unlikely . In the model, the challenge adds the stressed volume that 500 mL of crystalloid leaves in the vessels at the end of a 5-min infusion, and the HFpEF and normal scenarios can be compared directly.

Vasoreactivity

Acute vasodilator testing with inhaled NO identifies the few patients with idiopathic PAH who respond to calcium channel blockers. A positive test is a fall in mPAP of at least 10 mmHg to 40 mmHg or less, with an unchanged or increased cardiac output . Of 557 patients tested by Sitbon and colleagues, 12.6% met the earlier criteria, and only 6.8% were still improved on calcium channel blockers after a year . In the model, inhaled NO lowers PVR alone, so in the PAH scenarios the fall in mPAP is partly offset by the rise in cardiac output as the RV unloads.

Breathing

Spontaneous inspiration lowers intrathoracic pressure, and the tracing dips. A positive-pressure breath raises intrathoracic pressure, and the tracing rises . Pressures are read at end-expiration, when intrathoracic pressure is most constant . In tachypnea with active expiration the swings are larger and expiration itself raises intrathoracic pressure. With forceful breathing or dynamic hyperinflation, end-expiratory values can overestimate transmural pressure, and averaging over the breath is sometimes preferred .

The atrial waves

The a wave is atrial contraction at the end of diastole. The c wave is the tricuspid valve bulging into the atrium during isovolumic contraction. The x descent occurs as the atrium is pulled down while the ventricle empties. The v wave is atrial filling against the closed valve, and the y descent is atrial emptying after the valve opens . In atrial fibrillation there is no a wave . A cannon a wave occurs when the atrium contracts against a closed valve, as in AV dissociation, where these waves appear irregularly . In the simulation every beat has a cannon a wave so that the loop repeats. In severe TR, the v wave merges with the c wave into a large c-v wave, which is suggestive of severe TR but not specific for it .

Reading the wedge

PAWP is measured at end-expiration . PAWP stands in for the LV end-diastolic pressure, which in sinus rhythm is the mean of the a wave. In atrial fibrillation, PAWP is read 130 to 160 ms after the onset of the QRS, before the v wave . When the v waves are large, as in mitral regurgitation or with a stiff LA, the mean over the whole beat overestimates the LVEDP, and large v waves strongly suggest left heart disease . However, large v waves are neither sensitive nor specific for severe MR. Among 37 patients with severe MR, 43% had large v waves and 32% had trivial ones . With Acute severe mitral regurgitation selected, the mean on the monitor can be compared with the value at the a wave.

What comes from the model

Every wave comes from the model. The atria contract, which produces the a wave, its loss in atrial fibrillation, and the cannon a wave in AV dissociation, together with the fall in cardiac output when atrial contraction is lost or mistimed. Filling against the closed valve, with a small contribution from the leaflets bulging into the atrium, produces the c wave at the start of systole, and descent of the AV plane during ejection deepens the x descent. The regurgitant options open an orifice in the model, an EROA of 0.5 cm² into a normal-sized, stiffer LA for acute severe MR and an EROA of 0.9 cm² for severe TR, so the v waves and the fall in forward output are part of the same beat (see the Advanced page). The PAWP is the LA pressure smoothed with a 50 ms time constant and delayed by 60 ms, so in this model its waves arrive later than those of the LA and the small c wave disappears. Breathing is a fixed swing of 6 to 8 mmHg about every 4 s, timed to a whole number of beats. Waveform-only confirmation of a true wedge position is unreliable in practice .

What is measured

A standard study records the right atrial pressure (RAP), the RV pressure as the catheter passes through, the pulmonary artery systolic, diastolic, and mean pressures (PASP, PADP, and mPAP), the pulmonary artery wedge pressure (PAWP) as a surrogate for left atrial pressure, and the cardiac output by thermodilution or the direct Fick method. Stroke volume is cardiac output divided by heart rate. All pressures are read at end-expiration with the transducer zeroed at the level of the left atrium.

The 2022 ESC/ERS guidelines define pulmonary hypertension as mPAP above 20 mmHg at rest. Pre-capillary PH is defined by PAWP of 15 mmHg or less with PVR above 2 WU. Isolated post-capillary PH is PAWP above 15 mmHg with PVR of 2 WU or less, and combined post- and pre-capillary PH is PAWP above 15 mmHg with PVR above 2 WU . The 20 mmHg threshold was introduced by the 6th World Symposium task force, which kept a PVR threshold of 3 WU at the time . The diastolic pressure gradient was proposed as a marker of pulmonary vascular disease that is less affected by flow and left atrial pressure than the TPG . Resting mPAP in healthy subjects is 14.0 ± 3.3 mmHg .

Pulmonary resistance and compliance are inversely related, and their product, the RC time, is roughly constant across patients and during treatment , and in chronic thromboembolic disease it measured 0.49 ± 0.2 s . Compliance therefore falls steeply as PVR rises from normal, and lowering PVR yields a proportionally larger gain in compliance. PA compliance measured as SV/PP is an approximation that treats the pulmonary circulation as a single compliance .

In acute inferior myocardial infarction, a PAPi of 0.9 or less identified severe RV dysfunction with high sensitivity and specificity . PAPi has since been used more widely in cardiogenic shock and advanced heart failure. It is calculated as PA pulse pressure divided by RAP . Thresholds reported in these other settings were derived in different cohorts and are not interchangeable with the original value of 0.9.

Estimating RV–PA coupling invasively

The reference method is the multi-beat approach. RV pressure and volume are recorded with a conductance catheter while preload is reduced, for example by transient caval occlusion. The ESPVR is fitted to the end-systolic points, and its slope is Ees. Because it requires a conductance catheter and caval occlusion, the method is confined to research.

The single-beat method avoids caval occlusion. A sine wave is fitted to the isovolumic portions of a single high-fidelity RV pressure curve to estimate the maximal pressure the RV would generate if it contracted without ejecting (Pmax). Brimioulle and colleagues showed that the line from Pmax at end-diastolic volume, tangent to the end-systolic point, estimates the ESPVR . Because the same triangle defines both elastances, the coupling ratio simplifies to

Ees/Ea ≈ Pmax / Pes − 1

where mean PA pressure is often used for Pes . The method needs a high-fidelity RV pressure waveform. With a fluid-filled catheter, damping and whip distort the isovolumic upstroke on which the fit depends.

The effective PA elastance, estimated as Ea ≈ mPAP/SV or PASP/SV, summarizes RV afterload. It rises with PVR, with falling compliance, and with heart rate.

The left side, invasively

With an arterial line and a measured cardiac output, LV Ea can be approximated as 0.9 × SBP/SV . Ees cannot be measured with a PA catheter. Mixed venous saturation, lactate, and urine output indicate whether the circulation delivers adequate flow, which is a separate question from the efficiency of coupling. In septic shock, 21 of 25 patients were uncoupled before vasoactive drugs were started .
