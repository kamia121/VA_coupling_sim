Pulmonary artery catheter

Right heart catheterization measures pressure and flow directly: the pulmonary load in detail and, with assumptions, RV–PA coupling.

Float the catheter

The tracing is the pressure at the catheter tip, generated from the chosen patient's model beat. Advance the tip from RA to RV to PA to wedge. At the wedge, the dashed line is the true left atrial pressure. Change the atrial rhythm or valve lesion to see how the a, c, x, v and y waves change. Then switch on an artifact and compare what the monitor reads with the true pressure (faint grey line).

Waveforms by position

RA: low pressure, reported as a mean. RV: systolic peak equal to PA systolic, with a diastolic pressure near RA; report systolic/end-diastolic. PA: diastolic pressure stays well above RV diastolic because the pulmonic valve closes. Wedge: balloon occlusion. The tip sees pulmonary venous (left atrial) pressure through the capillary bed, so the wave is lower and smoother than the PA wave.

Damping

Overdamped (air bubble, clot, kinked or compliant tubing): the trace is rounded and systolic reads low. Underdamped (resonance, catheter whip): overshoot and ringing, so systolic reads high. Mean pressure is relatively insensitive to damping . The fast-flush test checks damping at the bedside .

Transducer height

Every pressure shifts by the hydrostatic column: a 10 cm levelling error changes readings by about 7.5 mmHg . In 196 patients, two commonly used zero levels differed by a median of 8.0 mmHg. The choice changed the proportion classified as having PH from 59% to 80% . A transducer below the reference reads high; one above reads low.

Breathing

Spontaneous inspiration lowers intrathoracic pressure and the tracing dips. A positive-pressure breath raises it and the tracing rises . Pressures are read at end-expiration, when intrathoracic pressure is most constant . With forceful breathing or dynamic hyperinflation, end-expiratory values can overestimate transmural pressure, and averaging over the breath is sometimes preferred .

Atrial waves: a, c, x, v, y

a: atrial contraction at the end of diastole. c: the tricuspid valve bulging into the atrium during isovolumic contraction. x: the atrium pulled down as the ventricle empties. v: atrial filling against the closed valve. y: emptying when the valve opens . Atrial fibrillation: no a wave . Cannon a: the atrium contracts against a closed valve, as in AV dissociation, where they come irregularly ; here every beat has one so the loop repeats. Severe TR: v merges with c into a large c-v wave, suggestive but not specific .

Reading the wedge

Measure at end-expiration . PAWP stands in for LV end-diastolic pressure, which in sinus rhythm is the mean of the a wave. In AF, read 130–160 ms after QRS onset, before the v wave . With large v waves (mitral regurgitation or a stiff LA), the mean over the whole beat overestimates LVEDP; large v waves strongly suggest left heart disease . They are neither sensitive nor specific for severe MR: in 37 patients with severe MR, 43% had large and 32% trivial v waves . Switch on Severe mitral regurgitation and compare the monitor's mean with the a wave.

What the model leaves out

The model has no atria that contract and no valve regurgitation. Its RA and LA pressures carry only the v wave and y descent. The a, c and x waves, the cannon a, and the MR and TR waves are added from a template timed to the model's valve events. Their sizes are illustrative, and the heart's output does not change with them. The wedge is the LA pressure smoothed (τ 50 ms) and delayed 60 ms, so here its waves arrive later than the LA's and the small c wave disappears. Breathing is a fixed ±6–8 mmHg swing about every 4 s (a whole number of beats). Waveform-only confirmation of a true wedge position is unreliable in practice .

What is measured

A standard study records right atrial pressure (RAP), RV pressure during passage, pulmonary artery systolic, diastolic and mean pressures (PASP, PADP, mPAP), pulmonary artery wedge pressure (PAWP) as a surrogate of left atrial pressure, and cardiac output by thermodilution or the direct Fick method. Stroke volume is cardiac output divided by heart rate. All pressures are read at end-expiration with the transducer zeroed at the level of the left atrium.

RC timePVR × PAC (× 0.06 to convert WU·mL/mmHg to seconds)Time constant of the pulmonary circulation
Pulmonary artery pulsatility index (PAPi)(PASP − PADP) / RAPRV output relative to its filling pressure

The 2022 ESC/ERS guidelines define pulmonary hypertension as mPAP above 20 mmHg at rest. Pre-capillary PH is defined by PAWP of 15 mmHg or less with PVR above 2 WU. Isolated post-capillary PH is PAWP above 15 mmHg with PVR of 2 WU or less, and combined post- and pre-capillary PH is PAWP above 15 mmHg with PVR above 2 WU . The 20 mmHg threshold was introduced by the 6th World Symposium task force, which kept a PVR threshold of 3 WU at the time . The diastolic pressure gradient was proposed as a marker of pulmonary vascular disease that is less affected by flow and left atrial pressure than the TPG . Resting mPAP in healthy subjects is 14.0 ± 3.3 mmHg .

Resistance and compliance are not independent in the lung. Their product, the RC time, is roughly constant across patients and during treatment , and in chronic thromboembolic disease it measured 0.49 ± 0.2 s . PAC falls steeply as PVR rises from normal; lowering PVR brings a proportionally larger gain in compliance. PA compliance measured as SV/PP is an approximation that treats the pulmonary circulation as a single compliance .

In acute inferior myocardial infarction, a PAPi of 0.9 or less identified severe RV dysfunction with high sensitivity and specificity . PAPi has since been used more widely in cardiogenic shock and advanced heart failure. It is calculated as PA pulse pressure divided by RAP . Thresholds from other settings come from different cohorts.

Estimating RV–PA coupling invasively

The reference method is the multi-beat approach: RV pressure and volume are recorded with a conductance catheter while preload is reduced (for example by transient caval occlusion), the ESPVR is fitted to the end-systolic points, and its slope is Ees. This is a research procedure.

The single-beat method avoids caval occlusion. A sine wave is fitted to the isovolumic portions of a single high-fidelity RV pressure curve to estimate the maximal pressure the RV would generate if it contracted without ejecting (Pmax). Brimioulle and colleagues showed that the line from Pmax at end-diastolic volume, tangent to the end-systolic point, estimates the ESPVR . Because the same triangle defines both elastances, the coupling ratio simplifies to

Ees/Ea ≈ Pmax / Pes − 1

where mean PA pressure is often used for Pes . It needs a high-fidelity RV pressure waveform; with a fluid-filled catheter, damping and whip distort the isovolumic upstroke the fit depends on.

Effective PA elastance, Ea ≈ mPAP/SV or PASP/SV, summarizes RV afterload; it rises with PVR, falling compliance and heart rate.

The left side, invasively

With an arterial line and a cardiac output, LV Ea can be approximated as 0.9·SBP/SV . Ees cannot be measured from a PA catheter. Mixed venous saturation, lactate and urine output tell you whether the coupled system is delivering enough flow. That is a different question from whether it is coupled efficiently, and septic vasoplegia is the clearest example of the two diverging .
